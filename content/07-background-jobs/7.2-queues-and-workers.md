# Queues and workers

A queue sits between the code that **asks** for work (producers) and the code that **does** it (consumers, or
workers). It stores jobs durably until a worker takes them.

```
API servers (producers) ──enqueue──▶ [ queue: job job job job ] ──▶ workers (consumers) ──▶ DB / services
```

Common choices: **SQS**, **RabbitMQ**, **Redis-based** queues (BullMQ, Sidekiq, Celery with Redis), **Kafka** (a log,
Module 15), or even a Postgres table with `SKIP LOCKED` for modest volumes.

## The lifecycle of a job

1. **Enqueue:** the producer sends `{type: "resize", photo_id: 42}`.
2. **Receive:** a worker takes the job. The queue hides it from other workers for a **visibility timeout** (say 60 s).
3. **Process:** the worker does the work.
4. **Acknowledge:** the worker tells the queue "done", and the job is deleted.
5. **No ack in time** (the worker crashed or was too slow): the job becomes visible again and another worker gets it.

Step 5 is what makes queues reliable, and it's also why a job can run **more than once** (lesson 7.3).

## Throughput and backlog

```
backlog growth/s = jobs enqueued/s − jobs completed/s
time to drain    = backlog ÷ (completion rate − arrival rate)
```

- **Completion rate** = workers × jobs per worker per second.
- If arrivals exceed completions, the backlog grows. That's fine for a short burst, a problem if it continues.
- The **age of the oldest job** is the key metric. It tells you how stale "eventually" has become.

The simulator shows this in the [Queue + workers preset](#/sandbox?preset=pipeline): run a 3x traffic spike and watch
the backlog grow, then drain.

## Workers and the database

More workers drain the queue faster, until the **database** becomes the limit. Workers that each write to Postgres
can overload it far more easily than user traffic, because they run flat out. Cap concurrency to what downstream
systems can absorb. The queue is there precisely so you **don't** have to process everything at once.

## Payloads

- Put **ids**, not data: `{photo_id: 42}`, not the whole photo. The worker fetches the current state.
- Include a **job id** and a **version** of the job format.
- Keep jobs small and focused. Split "process upload" into resize, scan and index jobs that can retry independently.

## Priorities

Use separate queues (and worker pools) for different urgencies: `critical` (password reset emails), `default`,
`bulk` (nightly exports). A flood of bulk jobs then can't delay the critical ones.

## Try it

Uploads arrive at 1,500/s. Each resize takes a worker 0.25 s. How many workers keep up? During a 4x burst lasting 60 s,
with 400 workers, how big does the backlog get, and how long does it take to drain after?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

One worker does 4 jobs/s, so 1,500 ÷ 4 = **375 workers** to keep up. With 400 workers, capacity is 1,600/s. During the
burst, arrivals are 6,000/s, so the backlog grows by 4,400/s × 60 s = **264,000 jobs**. Afterwards, the spare capacity
is 1,600 − 1,500 = 100/s, so draining takes **2,640 s ≈ 44 minutes**. You'd scale workers up during the burst if the
database can take it.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- Queues decouple producers from workers and store jobs until they're done.
- Visibility timeouts and acks make processing reliable, and make duplicates possible.
- Watch backlog and oldest-job age; drain time depends on spare capacity.
- Send ids, not data; cap workers to what downstream systems can absorb; separate queues by priority.
