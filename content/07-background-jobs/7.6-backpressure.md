# Backpressure

A queue absorbs bursts. But if work keeps arriving faster than it's done, the queue grows until something breaks:
memory, disk, or the patience of users waiting for results from two hours ago. **Backpressure** is how a system says
"slow down".

## The options when you're over capacity

| Strategy | What happens | Good for |
|----------|--------------|----------|
| **Buffer** | Queue it, process later | Short bursts with spare capacity afterwards |
| **Scale out** | Add workers | Sustained load, *if* downstream systems can take it |
| **Throttle producers** | Slow or reject new work at the source (429, a blocking enqueue) | Protecting the system and keeping latency bounded |
| **Shed load** | Drop low-value work | Non-essential work (analytics, recommendations) |
| **Degrade** | Do a cheaper version | "Show cached results", "skip the thumbnail" |

Real systems combine them: buffer short bursts, scale for sustained load, and shed or throttle beyond that.

## Bounded queues

An unbounded queue hides problems until it's huge. Set limits:
- a **maximum length or age**; past it, reject new jobs (or move them to an overflow path);
- an **alert on oldest-job age**, not only on length.

A queue of 10 million jobs, each now an hour stale, may be worse than rejecting work and telling users to retry.

## Autoscaling workers on the backlog

```
desired workers = ceil(backlog ÷ (jobs per worker per second × target drain time))
```

*Backlog 120,000; 4 jobs/s per worker; drain within 5 minutes → 120,000 ÷ (4 × 300) = 100 workers.*

Always cap the maximum at what the **database and other downstream systems** can absorb. Otherwise autoscaling just
moves the overload to the database.

## Backpressure through a pipeline

In a chain `A → queue → B → queue → C`, if C slows down, its queue fills, B should slow down, then A's queue fills,
and finally the API tells clients to back off. Each stage must **propagate** the pressure upstream instead of silently
buffering forever.

Streaming frameworks (Reactive Streams, Kafka consumers pulling at their own pace, TCP flow control) build this in:
consumers **pull** only what they can handle.

## Load shedding at the front door

When overloaded, reject early and cheaply:
- prioritise: checkout > browse > recommendations;
- return `503` or `429` with `Retry-After` quickly, rather than timing out slowly;
- protect health checks and admin endpoints from being shed.

## Try it

In the [Process uploads lab](#/labs/upload-pipeline), the reference design uses 8 workers. Try 20 workers on the same
database. What happens to reads, and why is fewer workers better here?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

20 workers × 400 = 8,000 jobs/s can drain the burst as fast as it arrives, but those writes push the database to
**100% busy**. User reads then queue behind them, and the read p99 jumps from ~50 ms to **~950 ms**. Any extra load
and reads start failing. With 8 workers the backlog grows to ~140,000 jobs during the burst and drains afterwards,
while the database stays ~60% busy and reads stay fast. The queue is doing its job: absorbing the burst.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- When work exceeds capacity: buffer, scale, throttle, shed or degrade.
- Bound queues and alert on the oldest job's age.
- Autoscale workers on backlog, capped by downstream capacity.
- Propagate backpressure upstream; reject early and cheaply at the front door.
