# Data that keeps growing

Traffic goes up and down. Data only goes up. Five years in, the orders table holds 2 billion rows, backups take 9
hours, and an index rebuild takes all weekend. Most of those rows haven't been read in years.

## Hot, warm and cold

Data is rarely accessed evenly over time:

| Tier | Example | Access | Where it belongs |
|------|---------|--------|------------------|
| **Hot** | Orders from the last 30 days | Constant reads and writes | Primary database, in memory |
| **Warm** | The last 1–2 years | Occasional lookups ("my order history") | Same database (partitioned) or cheaper storage |
| **Cold** | Older than 2 years | Rare: audits, legal, analytics | Object storage / data warehouse, compressed |

Keeping cold data in the hot database costs you **every day**: bigger indexes evicting hot data from RAM, slower
backups and restores, slower vacuum and migrations.

## Archiving

Move old rows out of the primary database on a schedule:

```
nightly job:
  copy orders older than 2 years → Parquet files in object storage (or a warehouse)
  verify row counts and checksums
  delete those rows in small batches (e.g. 10,000 at a time) to avoid long locks
```

- Users who need old data get it from the archive, which is slower, and that's fine for rare requests.
- Analytics query the warehouse, never the production database.
- **Legal retention** decides what you may delete and what you must keep, sometimes for 7+ years. Know it before
  designing.

## Retention by design

Some data shouldn't be kept at all:
- logs and raw events: 30–90 days, then aggregate;
- sessions, OTPs, temporary uploads: expire with TTLs;
- personal data: delete on account deletion (GDPR / DPDP-style rules).

Build expiry in from day one (an `expires_at` column, TTLs, lifecycle rules on buckets). Retrofitting deletion onto
billions of rows is painful.

## Plan growth with numbers

```
rows/day × bytes/row × days = size
2M orders/day × 1 KB × 365 = 730 GB/year (+ indexes ≈ ×2 → ~1.5 TB/year)
```

With a hot window of 90 days, the primary only needs ~180 GB of order data plus indexes. The rest moves to cheap
storage.

## The order of steps

Before reaching for sharding (lessons 9.3–9.4), try the cheaper steps:
1. **Archive** cold data.
2. **Partition** big tables inside one database (lesson 9.2).
3. **Scale up** the database and add replicas.
4. **Split by feature:** move a whole domain (e.g. analytics events, chat) to its own database.
5. **Shard**, only when one machine can't hold the hot data or handle the writes.

## Try it

A ride app stores every GPS point (every 4 s per active trip) in its main Postgres: 3 billion rows, growing 15M a day.
Trips older than 30 days are only viewed in "trip history" (a map of the route). What would you change?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

GPS points are high-volume and cold almost immediately. Keep a compact summary in Postgres (start, end, distance,
fare, a simplified polyline). Write raw points to a time-series store or object storage, partitioned by day, with a
retention policy (e.g. raw for 90 days, then only the simplified route). The main database shrinks by orders of
magnitude.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- Data only grows, and old data slows down everything around it.
- Separate hot, warm and cold data; archive cold data to cheap storage.
- Design retention and expiry from day one; know your legal requirements.
- Archive, partition, scale and split by feature before sharding.
