# Drawing boundaries

The hardest part of microservices isn't Kubernetes. It's deciding **where one service ends and another begins**. Good
boundaries make services independent; bad ones make every feature a cross-team, multi-service change.

## Split by business capability

Split along **what the business does**, not along technical layers.

| ❌ Technical layers | ✅ Business capabilities |
|---------------------|--------------------------|
| "API service", "database service", "validation service" | Catalogue, Cart, Orders, Payments, Shipping, Accounts |
| Every feature touches every service | Most features live inside one service |

## Bounded contexts

The same word means different things in different parts of a business. "Product" in the **catalogue** is a
description, photos and specs; in **inventory** it's a SKU with a stock count per warehouse; in **shipping** it's a
weight and dimensions.

A **bounded context** (from Domain-Driven Design) is a boundary within which a model and its words are consistent.
These make natural service boundaries: each context owns its own model of "product", and they share only an id.

## One owner per piece of data

**Each service owns its data, and nobody else touches its tables.**

```
❌ Orders service and Shipping service both UPDATE the same orders table
✅ Orders owns `orders`; Shipping learns about orders via Orders' API or its events,
   and keeps its own `shipments` table
```

A shared database couples services more tightly than any API: a schema change in one breaks the other, and you can't
scale or deploy them independently.

## Signs of a good boundary

- Most changes touch **one** service.
- Small, stable interfaces between services (a few calls or events).
- Each service can **answer its main questions with its own data**.
- One team can own it end to end.

## Signs of a bad boundary

- "Chatty" services: one user request means 15 calls back and forth.
- Two services always deployed together (a **distributed monolith**).
- Services reading each other's databases.
- Constant cross-service transactions.

## Duplicating data on purpose

Services often keep a **local copy** of data they need from others, updated by events:

```
Catalog publishes ProductUpdated(id, name, price)
Orders keeps product_name and price_at_purchase on each order line
```

Orders doesn't need to call Catalog to render an old order, and that's correct: it **should** show the price at the
time of purchase anyway.

## Try it

An e-commerce monolith has: products, stock levels, carts, orders, payments, shipments, user accounts, reviews and
recommendations. Group them into 4–6 services and say which data each owns.

<details>
<summary>One reasonable split</summary>

- **Catalogue:** products, categories, reviews (or reviews separately).
- **Inventory:** stock per SKU per warehouse, reservations.
- **Ordering:** carts, orders, order lines (with copied names and prices).
- **Payments:** payment intents, refunds (talks to the provider).
- **Fulfilment:** shipments, tracking.
- **Accounts:** users, addresses, auth.

Recommendations can be a separate service consuming events from the others.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- Split by business capability and bounded context, not by technical layer.
- Each service owns its data; no shared tables.
- Good boundaries mean most changes touch one service.
- Copy the data you need via events instead of calling or sharing databases.
