# Monolith first

A **monolith** is one deployable application containing all features. **Microservices** split those features into
separately deployed services that talk over the network. Microservices are fashionable. They're also a cost you
should only pay when you get something specific back.

## What a monolith gets right

- **One codebase, one deploy, one database.** Easy to understand, test and run.
- **Function calls instead of network calls:** fast, and they don't partially fail.
- **Transactions just work** across features (an order and its stock update in one `BEGIN … COMMIT`).
- **Refactoring is cheap:** moving code between modules is an IDE operation, not a migration.

Plenty of very large products ran (and run) on well-structured monoliths.

## What microservices give you

| Benefit | When it matters |
|---------|-----------------|
| **Independent deploys** | Many teams stepping on each other's releases |
| **Independent scaling** | One part needs 50x the resources of the rest (video transcoding vs settings pages) |
| **Fault isolation** | A crash in recommendations shouldn't take down checkout |
| **Technology freedom** | One part truly needs a different language or runtime |
| **Team autonomy** | Clear ownership for teams of ~5–10 engineers each |

Notice that most of these are **organisational**. Microservices mostly solve *people* scaling problems: many teams
working on one system.

## What microservices cost

- **Network calls everywhere:** latency, timeouts, retries, partial failure (Module 12).
- **No cross-service transactions:** you need sagas and the outbox (Module 16).
- **Distributed debugging:** tracing, correlation ids, central logs (lesson 11.5).
- **Operational load:** N pipelines, N dashboards, N on-call rotations, service discovery, versioned APIs.
- **Wrong boundaries are expensive:** splitting along the wrong seams gives a *distributed monolith*, with all the
  costs and none of the benefits.

## The modular monolith

Get most of the structure without the network:

```
app/
  orders/      (public interface + internal code + its own tables)
  payments/
  catalog/
  users/
```

- Each module has a **public interface**; others may not reach into its internals or its tables.
- Enforce the boundaries with tooling (import rules, separate schemas).
- When a module truly needs independence, it's already shaped like a service: **extract** it.

## When to split

Split a module out when you can point to a concrete pain:
- a team regularly blocked by others' deploys;
- one component's scaling needs dwarfing the rest;
- a failure in one area repeatedly taking down unrelated features;
- a clear, stable boundary with a small interface.

## Try it

A 6-engineer startup with one product wants to "start with microservices to be ready for scale": 12 services, each with
its own database. Give your advice.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Start with a **modular monolith**: 6 engineers can't sensibly own 12 services (pipelines, on-call, versioning), and
the domain boundaries aren't known yet, so they'll likely be wrong. Keep clear module boundaries and per-module tables
so services can be extracted later, when a specific pain (team contention, scaling, isolation) justifies one.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- Monoliths are simple, fast and transactional. They're a fine default.
- Microservices mainly solve organisational scaling, and add network, consistency and operational costs.
- A modular monolith gives you boundaries without the network.
- Split when a concrete pain justifies it, along stable boundaries.
