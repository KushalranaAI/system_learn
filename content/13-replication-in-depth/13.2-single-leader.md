# Single-leader replication

The most common design (Postgres, MySQL, MongoDB replica sets, most managed databases): **one leader accepts all
writes**, and followers apply the leader's change log in the same order.

```
clients ──writes──▶ Leader ──log──▶ Follower 1
                          └──log──▶ Follower 2
clients ──reads──▶ leader or followers
```

Because one node orders all writes, there are **no write conflicts**. That's its great strength.

## When is a write acknowledged?

| Mode | Leader waits for | Latency | Data loss if the leader dies |
|------|------------------|---------|-------------------------------|
| **Async** | Nothing | Lowest | Writes not yet shipped are lost |
| **Sync (all)** | Every follower | Highest; one slow follower stalls writes | None |
| **Semi-sync** | At least one follower | One extra round trip | None, if that follower is promoted |
| **Quorum** (Raft-based DBs) | A majority | One round trip to the closest majority | None |

Most production setups use **one synchronous follower** (or a quorum) in the same region for durability, plus async
followers for read scale and other regions.

## Replication lag, revisited

Async followers lag behind. Three consistency guarantees help users cope (lesson 5.3):

| Guarantee | Promise | Technique |
|-----------|---------|-----------|
| **Read-your-writes** | You see your own changes | Read from the leader for a while after writing, or wait for the follower to reach your write's log position |
| **Monotonic reads** | You never see time go backwards | Pin each user to one follower |
| **Consistent prefix** | You see writes in the order they happened | Followers apply the log in order (true for single-leader) |

## Adding a follower

1. Take a consistent **snapshot** of the leader (tied to a log position).
2. Copy it to the new follower.
3. Stream the log **from that position** until caught up.
4. Start serving reads.

No downtime, and it's the same mechanism as recovering a follower that fell behind.

## Limits

- **Write throughput** is one machine's. To scale writes you need partitioning (Modules 9 and 14).
- **Cross-region writes** all travel to the leader's region.
- **Failover** is the risky moment (13.3).

## Try it

A Postgres leader in Mumbai has one sync follower in another Mumbai zone and two async followers in Singapore. The
Mumbai zone with the leader fails. What data can be lost, and which follower should be promoted?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Promote the **sync follower** in the other Mumbai zone: it has every acknowledged write, so nothing is lost. Promoting
a Singapore async follower could lose the most recent writes (whatever hadn't replicated yet), and moves the write
path across regions.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- One leader orders all writes, so there are no write conflicts.
- Choose sync, semi-sync or quorum acknowledgement for durability; async for speed and distance.
- Read-your-writes, monotonic reads and consistent prefix make lag tolerable.
- Write throughput is limited to one leader; failover is the risky part.
