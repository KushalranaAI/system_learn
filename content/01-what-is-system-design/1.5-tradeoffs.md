# Everything is a tradeoff

Every design choice buys something and pays for it with something else. Senior
engineers aren't the ones who know the "best" database. They're the ones who
can say **what each option costs** for *this* problem.

## Tradeoffs you'll meet again and again

| You gain | You pay | Example |
|----------|---------|---------|
| **Speed** (cache) | Staleness, memory cost, invalidation bugs | Product prices cached for 60 s |
| **Availability** (replicas) | Consistency: copies can lag | A new post missing on refresh |
| **Strong consistency** | Latency, and availability during network splits | Bank balance checks the leader |
| **Headroom** (run at 50% CPU) | Money | Twice the servers you strictly need |
| **Flexibility** (microservices) | Operational complexity, network hops | 12 deploy pipelines instead of 1 |
| **Simplicity** (one big server) | A scaling ceiling, a single point of failure | Great until it isn't |
| **Normalised data** | More joins on reads | Clean writes, slower dashboards |
| **Denormalised data** | Duplicate writes, drift risk | Fast reads, harder updates |
| **Async processing** | Delay, harder debugging | "Your video is processing…" |

## How to choose

1. **Go back to the requirements.** Which non-functional requirement matters
   most? If it's "never lose an order", consistency wins over latency for that
   path.
2. **Choose per path, not per system.** Viewing a product can be cached and a
   bit stale. Paying for it can't. One system often makes opposite choices on
   different endpoints.
3. **Prefer reversible choices.** Adding a cache later is easy. Splitting a
   monolith's database later is hard. Spend your thinking on decisions that are
   hard to undo.
4. **Pick the simplest option that meets the target.** Extra parts are extra
   things that break.

## Write it down: an ADR

An **Architecture Decision Record** is a short note per decision:

```markdown
# ADR-003: Cache product pages in Redis for 60 seconds

## Context
The product page gets 20k reads/s at peak; Postgres tops out near 8k.

## Decision
Cache-aside in Redis, key `product:{id}`, TTL 60 s.

## Consequences
+ DB load drops ~90%, p99 falls from 400 ms to ~20 ms.
− Price changes can take up to 60 s to show. Accepted: checkout re-reads the
  price from the DB.
− One more component to run and monitor.
```

Six months later, this note saves an hour of "why is this cached?!"

## Try it

A social app shows a **like count** under each post. Option A: read the exact
count from the database every time. Option B: keep an approximate count in a
cache and sync it to the database every 10 s.

Write a mini ADR choosing one. Include at least one "+" and two "−".

<details>
<summary>One good answer</summary>

Choose **B** for display. Nobody notices 1,204 vs 1,207, and a viral post would
otherwise hammer one database row.
+ Handles viral spikes; cheap reads.
− Counts lag by up to 10 s; a cache crash can lose up to 10 s of likes unless
  likes are also logged durably.
The *list of who liked* (used for "you liked this") stays exact in the
database.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- Every choice gains something and pays with something.
- Decide per request path, guided by the most important requirement.
- Spend effort on hard-to-reverse decisions; keep the rest simple.
- Record decisions and their costs in ADRs.
