# Uber: real-time dispatch

A ride-hailing marketplace is a constantly changing map. Every few seconds, each driver's phone reports a new
location, riders open the app and ask "how far is the nearest car?", and the dispatch system has to make a good match
within seconds. The data is **write-heavy, local and short-lived**: a location from two minutes ago is useless.

## The problem at their scale

| Number | Value | When |
|--------|-------|------|
| Trips | about 11.3 billion in the year, about 31 million a day | 2024 |
| Trips in Q4 | about 3.1 billion | Q4 2024 |
| Trips per day, Q1 average | about 28 million | Q1 2024 |
| Monthly active platform consumers | 171 million | Q4 2024 |
| Geo-index design target | 1 million writes/s, many times that in reads | 2015 (QCon talk) |

All of it is **local**. A rider in Mumbai never needs to know about a driver in Lima. That makes geography the natural
shard key, but cities are uneven: one block in central London can be busier than a whole small town.

## The architecture

```
 driver apps ── location every few seconds ──►  supply service ──┐
                                                                 ▼
                                       geo index, sharded by cell id across a ring of nodes
                                       (in 2015: Google S2 cells + Ringpop consistent hashing)
                                                                 ▲
 rider app ── "request ride" ──► demand service ──► DISCO (dispatch optimisation)
                                                     1. cells covering the pickup radius
                                                     2. candidate drivers from those cells
                                                     3. rank by ETA on the road graph, not distance
                                                     4. offer to driver, wait, fall back to next
 marketplace: supply & demand counted per H3 hexagon ──► surge multiplier per hexagon
```

## Key decisions and why

**1. Index the earth with cells, and shard by cell.** In his 2015 QCon talk, Matt Ranney described DISCO using Google's
**S2** library: the sphere is split into cells, each identified by a 64-bit integer. A location update becomes a
write to the shard that owns its cell id. A "drivers near me" query becomes a read of a handful of cells around the
pickup. This is the [geohash/quadtree](#/lesson/19.9) idea applied to sharding ([lesson 9.3](#/lesson/9.3)).

**2. Stateful services on a gossip-based hash ring.** Uber built and open-sourced **Ringpop**: nodes discover each
other with a SWIM-style gossip protocol ([lesson 19.6](#/lesson/19.6)) and own key ranges by consistent hashing
([lesson 14.2](#/lesson/14.2)). A request for cell X is forwarded to whichever node owns X, so the latest driver
locations stay in memory, without a database round trip per update.

**3. Hexagons for the marketplace (H3).** For pricing and analysis, Uber built **H3**, a hexagonal hierarchical grid
(open-sourced 2018). Hexagons have one distance to every neighbour, where squares have two (edge and corner). That
makes "smooth demand over neighbouring areas" simple. H3 has 16 resolutions, and each finer level has cells about one
seventh the area. Uber computes **surge pricing** from supply and demand measured within hexagons in each city.

**4. Match on ETA, and look slightly ahead.** The nearest car in a straight line may be across a river. DISCO ranks
candidates by road-network ETA. It can also consider drivers finishing a trip nearby, who might arrive sooner than an
idle driver further away. That was an improvement over the first system, which only considered idle drivers.

## What went wrong along the way

- **Fixed-size cells make uneven shards.** Supply and demand pile up in city centres (central London was Ranney's
  example), so a few cells take a large share of the updates. Cell size is a trade-off: too large means hot shards and
  big scans, too small means many cells per query. Resolution has to suit the density.
- **Stateful services are harder to operate.** Holding data in memory on a hash ring means that membership changes,
  deploys and node failures move ownership, and in-flight state must be rebuilt from fresh updates. Ringpop works
  because locations expire in seconds anyway.
- **Distance is not time.** Straight-line matching produced bad pickups, so ETA on the road graph became core
  infrastructure.

## Try it

Match riders to drivers in one city. There are **20,000 online drivers**, each sending a location every 4 seconds, and
**60 ride requests per second** at peak. Pickup radius is about 2 km. Use H3-style cells (resolution 8 cells average
about 0.74 km²; resolution 9 about 0.1 km²). Describe the index, the query and how you stop two riders getting the same
driver.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

**Writes:** 20,000 / 4 s = **5,000 location updates/s**. Store `cell_id → {driver_id: (lat, lng, status, ts)}` in
memory, sharded by cell id. Drop entries older than about 15 s.

**Resolution:** a 2 km radius covers about 12.6 km². At resolution 8 that's about 17 cells (one cell plus two rings of
neighbours = 19). At resolution 9 it's over 100 cells. Use **resolution 8** for the query, perhaps resolution 9 in the
dense centre.

**Query per request:** pickup cell + 2 rings of neighbours, which is about 19 lookups and a few hundred candidates
downtown. Filter by status = available, take the nearest 10-20 by straight line, then rank by **road ETA**.
60 requests/s x about 19 cell reads is about 1,100 reads/s, which is easy.

**One driver, one rider:** when offering, take a short **lease** on the driver (e.g. `SET driver:123 offer:req9 NX
EX 15`, see [lesson 12.7](#/lesson/12.7)). If the driver declines or the lease expires, release it and offer the next
candidate. A small improvement: collect requests for 1-2 seconds and match them as a batch, which avoids greedy
choices that leave a later rider with no car.
</details>
