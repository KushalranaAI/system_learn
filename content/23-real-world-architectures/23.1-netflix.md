# Netflix: streaming to hundreds of millions

Netflix splits its system in two. The **control plane** (sign-in, browsing, recommendations, deciding which server
plays your video) runs on AWS. The **data plane** (the video bytes themselves) runs on Netflix's own CDN, Open Connect,
whose servers sit inside internet providers' buildings. The rest of the design follows from that split.

## The problem at their scale

| Number | Value | When |
|--------|-------|------|
| Paid memberships | about 302 million | end of 2024 (10-K) |
| Share of video served by Open Connect | 100% | Netflix ISP page |
| Open Connect locations | close to 1,000 | Netflix ISP page |
| Traffic over direct ISP connections | close to 90% | Netflix ISP page |
| Throughput of one appliance | 8 Gbps (2012) to over 90 Gbps (2016) | Netflix ISP page |
| Peak concurrent streams, live event | 65 million | Paul vs Tyson, November 2024 |

Video is heavy. One HD stream is a few megabits per second, so tens of millions of evening viewers add up to
hundreds of terabits per second. No single data centre or cloud region can serve that. API calls are tiny by
comparison, and they are the part that changes often.

## The architecture

```
                 CONTROL PLANE (AWS, 3+ regions)                 DATA PLANE (Open Connect)
 device ──HTTPS──► edge gateway (Zuul) ──► microservices          ┌──────────────────────────┐
   │               (login, browse, recs, play API)                │ OCA inside your ISP      │
   │                         │                                    │ (cache appliance)        │
   │                         └─ "steering": picks the best OCAs ─►│ filled overnight with    │
   │                            for this device and title         │ what people will watch   │
   │                                                              └──────────┬───────────────┘
   └──────────────── video segments over HTTP ────────────────────────────────┘
                                                  fallback: OCAs at internet exchange points
```

1. You press play. The play API on AWS checks your plan and device, then returns a list of Open Connect appliances
   (OCAs) that hold the file, ranked by health and network distance.
2. The player fetches video segments straight from an OCA, usually inside your own ISP, and switches bitrate as your
   bandwidth changes.
3. OCAs report their health back to the control plane, so steering avoids busy or broken ones.

## Key decisions and why

**1. Build a CDN and put it inside ISPs.** Netflix gives qualifying ISPs appliances for free. The ISP saves on transit
costs, and Netflix gets servers one hop from the viewer. Netflix knows its catalogue and can predict tomorrow's
viewing, so it **pre-positions** popular titles during off-peak fill windows instead of waiting for a cache miss.
Most of "caching" here is planned distribution. See [lesson 3.7](#/lesson/3.7) for CDN basics.

**2. Keep the control plane in the cloud.** After a 2008 database corruption stopped DVD shipping for three days,
Netflix moved its services to AWS. It completed the move in January 2016. The control plane needs elastic compute and
fast deploys, not raw bandwidth, so the cloud suits it.

**3. Break things on purpose.** Chaos Monkey (described in 2011) kills random instances during business hours, so
every service must survive losing one. Chaos Gorilla removes a whole availability zone, and Chaos Kong a whole region.
Failure stops being rare, and recovery paths get regular use. See [lesson 18.6](#/lesson/18.6).

**4. Make regional evacuation fast.** Netflix runs active-active across AWS regions (US-East, US-West, EU). If one
region degrades, its traffic moves to the other two. In 2018 Netflix described cutting evacuation from about **45
minutes to about 7**. Most of the old time went to scaling the "savior" regions on demand. The fix, Project Nimble,
keeps hidden shadow capacity on reserved instances it already paid for, injects it in about four minutes, then flips
DNS. See [lesson 18.4](#/lesson/18.4) and [lesson 18.5](#/lesson/18.5).

## What went wrong along the way

- **2008:** a corrupted database in Netflix's own data centre halted shipping for three days. This led to the cloud
  migration and the habit of assuming any single component can fail.
- **Christmas Eve 2012:** an AWS load balancer outage in US-East took down streaming on many devices for hours. It is
  why Netflix invested in multi-region failover and Chaos Kong.
- **November 2024:** the Paul vs Tyson fight peaked at 65 million concurrent streams, and many viewers saw buffering.
  On-demand video can be pre-positioned overnight. A live event cannot: every viewer wants the same seconds of
  video at the same time, and the system had been tuned for a different load shape.

## Try it

A new season premieres worldwide at the same minute. You expect **30 million concurrent streams** at peak. Average
bitrate is 5 Mbps (most viewers on HD, some on mobile). Each cache appliance serves 90 Gbps at full load, and you
never run them above 60%. Estimate peak egress and the number of appliances needed. What can the control plane do to
help? (Estimation method: [lesson 1.4](#/lesson/1.4).)

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

**Egress:** 30,000,000 x 5 Mbps = 150,000,000 Mbps = **150 Tbps**.

**Appliances:** each one usefully serves 90 x 0.6 = 54 Gbps. 150,000 Gbps / 54 Gbps is about **2,800 appliances**,
spread across the regions where viewers are, plus headroom for failed units and uneven geography. With 4K viewers
(about 15 Mbps) the number roughly doubles.

**What the control plane can do:**
- Pre-position the episode on every relevant OCA days ahead, so there are no cache misses at launch.
- Steer each player to a lightly loaded OCA, spilling to exchange-point OCAs when an ISP's are full.
- Under pressure, cap the starting bitrate for a few minutes. Lower quality is better than buffering.
- Stagger non-essential work (artwork prefetch, recommendations) away from the launch minute.
</details>
