# Amazon DynamoDB: predictable at any scale

DynamoDB's promise isn't "fast". It's **the same latency at 10 requests per second and at 100 million**. The 2022
USENIX ATC paper by its engineers explains what that took: partitioning, replication, and a decade of work on one
problem, which is that real workloads are not uniform. Some keys are much hotter than others, and the system has to
cope without the customer noticing.

## The problem at their scale

Amazon's own Prime Day traffic (Alexa, Amazon.com, fulfilment centres) is the public benchmark:

| Prime Day | DynamoDB peak | Notes |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| 2021 | 89.2 million requests/s | 66-hour event, trillions of calls (ATC paper) |
| 2022 | 105.2 million requests/s | AWS blog |
| 2024 | 146 million requests/s | AWS blog |
| 2025 | 151 million requests/s | "tens of trillions" of calls |
| 2026 | 192 million requests/s | over 59 trillion requests, June 23-26 |

All with single-digit-millisecond responses. The SLA is 99.99% availability for regional tables and 99.999% for
global tables (as of the 2022 paper).

## The architecture

```
 client ─► request router (stateless, many) ─► "which partition holds this key?"
              │  auth, admission control              partition map cache ◄── MemDS (in-memory metadata)
              ▼
     partition = key range of a table, replicated 3 ways across availability zones
     ┌─────────────── replication group (Multi-Paxos) ───────────────┐
     │ leader replica (AZ-a)   follower (AZ-b)   follower (AZ-c)      │   writes: leader logs,
     │  B-tree + write-ahead log                  (or log-only replica)│   quorum of 2/3 acks
     └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
     storage nodes host thousands of replicas from many tables and customers
     autoadmin: moves replicas off hot nodes, splits partitions, repairs failures
```

## Key decisions and why

**1. Partition by key, replicate with Paxos.** A table is split into partitions by hashing the partition key
([lesson 14.1](#/lesson/14.1)). Each partition is a replication group across three availability zones that elects a
leader with Multi-Paxos ([lesson 19.7](#/lesson/19.7) covers Raft, a close cousin). Writes need a quorum. Strongly
consistent reads go to the leader, eventually consistent reads to any replica.

**2. Throughput is a per-partition budget, and that was the hard part.** Originally, a table's provisioned throughput
was divided evenly among its partitions. The paper's example: with a 1,000 WCU cap per partition, a 3,200 WCU table gets
4 partitions of 800 WCU each. But traffic is rarely even. A partition holding a hot key throttled while the table as a
whole sat idle. Splitting a partition for **size** made this worse, because each child got half the throughput
("throughput dilution"). The fixes came in layers:
- **Bursting:** a partition may use unused capacity saved over the last 300 seconds, if the node has room.
- **Adaptive capacity:** boost partitions that are throttling while the table is under its total. This removed over
  99.99% of throttling caused by skew, but it reacted only after throttling had already started.
- **Global admission control (GAC):** move admission out of the partition into a service that tracks the **table's**
  consumption with token buckets. Partitions can always burst, within node limits.
- **Split for consumption:** a partition that stays hot is split, at a point chosen from the keys it has actually
  seen. Splits take minutes. This does not help a **single hot item** or keys written in sequence, and DynamoDB
  detects those patterns and doesn't try.
- **On-demand tables:** no capacity to plan. Partitions are provisioned from observed consumption.

See [lesson 14.3](#/lesson/14.3) and [lesson 19.10](#/lesson/19.10).

**3. Constant work for metadata.** Routers cached each table's partition map and hit the cache about 99.75% of the
time. That is good on average but **bimodal**: when caches were cold (new routers added), every request became a
metadata lookup, and metadata traffic could jump to 75%. The fix was **MemDS**, an in-memory metadata store. Routers
refresh from it asynchronously **on every hit**, so its load is constant whatever the hit rate. It does more work on
normal days in exchange for no surprises on bad ones.

**4. Small blast radius.** AWS builds services as many independent cells and zones, so one failure affects only a slice
of customers ([lesson 18.3](#/lesson/18.3)). DynamoDB's partitions, replication groups and per-AZ placement apply the
same idea at the storage layer.

## What went wrong along the way

- **20 September 2015, US-East:** a brief network disruption made storage servers re-request their membership data
  from the metadata service all at once. Global secondary indexes had made those responses much larger, the metadata
  service lacked capacity, and responses arrived after the servers' time limit, so they retried. The customer error
  rate reached about 55%. Fixes included more metadata capacity, fewer and slower requests, and plans to split the
  service. The pattern (cold caches, retries, an overloaded central dependency) is the same one MemDS was later built
  to avoid ([lesson 12.2](#/lesson/12.2)).
- **19-20 October 2025, US-East-1:** a race between two components of DynamoDB's automated DNS management left the
  regional endpoint with **no IP addresses**. DynamoDB errors lasted about 2 hours 52 minutes, and many AWS services
  that depend on it failed too. See [lesson 20.1](#/lesson/20.1).
- **Throughput dilution** confused customers for years. Many over-provisioned "just in case", which is why GAC and
  on-demand mode exist.

## Try it

A flash-sale table uses partition key `sale_id`. One sale gets **40,000 writes/s** (people adding the item to carts)
and 100,000 reads/s of the sale's stock counter. One partition handles about 1,000 WCU and 3,000 RCU. What happens,
and how do you spread the hot key?

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

**What happens:** every write targets the same partition key, so they all hit **one partition**. 40,000 WCU against
about 1,000 means heavy throttling. Split for consumption can't help, because it is a single item. Adaptive capacity
and GAC can't exceed one partition's physical limit.

**Spread writes: write sharding.** Use `sale_id#N` with N = random 0..63 (64 shards, about 625 writes/s each). Each
cart-add goes to a random shard. To read a total, query all 64 and sum, or keep a rolled-up total updated every second
by a stream consumer ([lesson 12.8](#/lesson/12.8)).

**Spread reads:** 100,000 reads/s of one counter belongs in a cache (DAX or your own) with a 1-second TTL. Or read
the rolled-up item, which you can also replicate under several keys (`stock#0..9`) and read at random.

**Stock correctness:** don't decrement one hot item per purchase. Allocate stock to shards (each shard owns 1/64 of the
units), and decrement with a conditional write `stock > 0` on the shard. If a shard runs out, try another
([lesson 14.3](#/lesson/14.3)).
</details>
