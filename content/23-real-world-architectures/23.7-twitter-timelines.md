# Twitter/X: building timelines

Your home timeline is the latest posts from everyone you follow, merged and sorted. Computing that from scratch on
every refresh means querying hundreds of accounts. Computing it in advance means one post from a celebrity triggers
tens of millions of writes. Twitter's answer, described by Raffi Krikorian in 2012-2013 talks, is the standard
reference for **fan-out on write vs fan-out on read**.

No sandbox scenario for this one. The [ranked feed lab](#/labs/ranked-feed) and the
[news feed interview scenario](#/sandbox?preset=iv-news-feed) cover the same trade-off.

## The problem at their scale (2012-2013)

| Number | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Active users | about 150 million |
| Timeline reads | about 300,000 queries/s |
| Tweets written | about 400 million/day, about 5,000/s on average, over 12,000/s during big events |
| Timeline deliveries (fan-out inserts) | about 30 billion/day |
| Followers of the biggest accounts | Lady Gaga about 31 million, Obama about 23 million |
| One-second record | 143,199 tweets/s (3 August 2013, Castle in the Sky airing in Japan) |

Reads outnumber writes about **50 to 1**. That ratio decided the design: do the work on the write path, once, so
the 300K reads/s are cheap.

## The architecture

```
 POST tweet ─► write API ─► tweet store (by tweet id)
                   │
                   └─► fan-out service: look up the author's followers (social graph service)
                          for each follower active in the last 30 days:
                              LPUSH timeline:<follower> tweet_id     (Redis, x3 replicas)
                              LTRIM to 800 entries

 GET home timeline ─► timeline service ─► Redis list of ~800 tweet ids ─► hydrate ids
                                           (+ merge in tweets from very large accounts, read time)
                                           ─► tweet + user caches ─► response
```

A timeline entry is tiny, roughly (tweet id, author id, a few flags). The tweet text is fetched ("hydrated") from a
cache at read time, so a deleted or edited tweet doesn't need to be removed from millions of lists.

## Key decisions and why

**1. Fan-out on write into in-memory timelines.** Each tweet is pushed into every follower's Redis timeline. A read
becomes one list fetch plus hydration. Krikorian's numbers: timeline service median about 5 ms, p99 about 100 ms. See
[lesson 15.5](#/lesson/15.5).

**2. Bound the cost.** Timelines are **capped at 800 entries**, and kept in RAM only for users **active in the last 30
days**. An inactive user's timeline is rebuilt from scratch on their next visit, which is slow but rare. Three Redis
replicas per timeline protect against losing a node. These limits made the memory bill (a few terabytes of RAM)
manageable.

**3. Accept slow delivery for huge fan-outs, then go hybrid.** Delivering to 1 million followers took about **3.5 s
at the median**, but up to **5 minutes at p99**. That caused visible problems: replies to a celebrity's tweet could
arrive before the tweet itself. The fix is **hybrid fan-out**: don't fan out tweets from accounts with huge follower
counts. Fetch them at read time and merge them into the precomputed list. A few hundred celebrity reads per timeline
request are cheaper than 30 million writes per celebrity tweet. See [lesson 14.3](#/lesson/14.3) for the same
"celebrity problem" in partitioning.

**4. Rebuild the platform for spikes.** After overloads during the 2010 World Cup ("fail whale"), Twitter moved from a
Ruby on Rails monolith to JVM services with clear boundaries. In August 2013 a 143,199 tweets/s spike, about 25x the
average, passed without an outage.

## What went wrong along the way

- **2010 World Cup:** goal-time tweet spikes overwhelmed the monolith repeatedly. This drove the service split
  ([lesson 11.2](#/lesson/11.2)).
- **Out-of-order delivery** for big accounts: a 5-minute p99 fan-out means followers see replies first. Hybrid fan-out
  exists because of this.
- **Write amplification:** one tweet from a 30M-follower account becomes 30M x 3 replica inserts. Fan-out queues can
  fall behind during events, and timelines go stale exactly when people are watching most.

## Try it

Decide who gets fan-out on read. Your app: 200M daily users, 2,000 timeline reads/s per million users at peak (so
400K reads/s), and each user follows about 300 accounts. One fan-out insert costs about 1 unit, one read-time fetch
of an account's recent posts costs about 2 units (it hits a per-author cache). An account with F followers posting
once costs F units on write. Where would you set the follower threshold, and what does a timeline read cost?

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

**Write cost** for a post: F inserts. An account with 10M followers posting 10 times a day costs 100M inserts/day,
over 1,000/s on its own, and delivery to the tail takes minutes.

**Read cost** of pulling instead: each reader who follows that account pays about 2 units per timeline read. If its
10M followers read their timeline 10 times a day, that's 10M x 10 x 2 = **200M units/day**. That is the same order as
fan-out on write, but with **no delivery delay** and no write burst.

The crossover depends on post rate vs read rate, so pick a threshold such as **F > 1 million** (or post rate x F above
a budget). In practice:
- Below the threshold: fan-out on write. Cheap, precomputed.
- Above it (a few thousand accounts): fan-out on read. Each timeline read merges the precomputed list with recent
  posts from the few big accounts this user follows. Most users follow under 10 of them, so the merge costs about 20
  units.
- Also skip fan-out to inactive followers. Rebuild their timeline when they return.

A typical read is then 1 list fetch + about 10 celebrity fetches + hydration, a few milliseconds from cache.
</details>
