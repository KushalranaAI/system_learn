# YouTube: video at planetary scale

Netflix serves a curated catalogue it can pre-position. YouTube takes anything anyone uploads, makes it watchable on
every device within minutes, and serves a very long tail: most videos are rarely watched, and a few are watched by
hundreds of millions. That gives three separate problems: **processing** (transcoding), **metadata** (a huge sharded
MySQL), and **delivery** (edge caches).

No case-study sandbox scenario for this one. The [video interview scenario](#/sandbox?preset=iv-video) and the
[video platform lab](#/labs/video-platform) cover the same path.

## The problem at their scale

| Number | Value | When |
|--------|-------|------|
| Video uploaded | more than 500 hours every minute | stated 2019, repeated 2021 |
| Same figure, earlier | 400 hours/minute | 2015 |
| Encoding cost of VP9 vs H.264 | about 5x the compute | YouTube, 2021 |
| Gain from custom transcoding chips (Argos VCU) | up to 20-33x compute efficiency | YouTube, 2021 |
| Vitess (MySQL sharding) | built at YouTube in 2010, CNCF graduated November 2019 | |

500 hours a minute is 30,000 hours of new video every hour. Each upload becomes **many** files: several resolutions
times several codecs, cut into segments for adaptive streaming.

## The architecture

```
 UPLOAD                       PROCESS                                   SERVE
 creator ─► resumable   ─►  original in blob store ─► job queue ─►  transcode workers / VCUs
            upload                                     │               split into chunks, encode in parallel
            (chunks)                                   │               ladder: 144p 240p 360p 480p 720p 1080p 4K
                                                       │               codecs: H.264, VP9, AV1
                                                       ▼
                                                   checks: copyright (Content ID), safety, thumbnails
                                                       │
 metadata (title, owner, views, comments) ─► MySQL behind Vitess (sharded) ◄── "video ready" event
                                                       │
 viewer ─► watch page/API ─► picks edge ─► edge cache (Google's edge + caches inside ISPs)
                                              hit: serve segments    miss: fill from origin
```

## Key decisions and why

**1. Transcode into a ladder, in parallel chunks.** Devices and networks vary, so each video is encoded at several
resolution/bitrate steps (a "ladder"), and the player switches between them as bandwidth changes (adaptive streaming,
HLS/DASH). The video is split into chunks that encode in parallel across many machines, and the results are stitched
back together, so a long upload finishes in minutes, not hours. See [lesson 7.7](#/lesson/7.7) and
[lesson 7.5](#/lesson/7.5).

**2. Spend compute where views are.** Encoding is expensive and most videos get few views. Better codecs (VP9, AV1)
save bandwidth on every view but cost more to encode. YouTube said VP9 needs about 5x the compute of H.264. The
sensible policy is a fast, cheap encode first so the video is watchable, then more expensive codecs for videos that
turn out to be popular. In 2021 YouTube announced its own chip, the **Argos VCU**, with up to 20-33x better compute
efficiency than its previous optimised software setup.

**3. Shard MySQL behind a proxy: Vitess.** YouTube's MySQL went the usual route: one primary, then replicas for reads,
then sharding when writes outgrew the primary. Rather than put shard routing in application code, YouTube built
**Vitess** (2010): a proxy layer (VTGate) that routes queries to the right shard, a sidecar per MySQL instance
(VTTablet) that pools connections and guards against harmful queries, and tooling for **online resharding**. The
application talks to what looks like one database. See [lesson 9.3](#/lesson/9.3) and
[lesson 14.5](#/lesson/14.5).

**4. Cache at the edge, close to viewers.** Popular segments are served from Google's edge network and caches placed
inside ISPs (Google Global Cache), the same idea as Netflix's Open Connect. The long tail is served from deeper in the
network. See [lesson 3.7](#/lesson/3.7).

## What went wrong along the way

- **Shard logic leaks everywhere** when the app chooses shards itself. Vitess exists because of that experience. It
  moved routing into a proxy, so resharding no longer meant changing application code.
- **CPU transcoding cost grew with every upload and every new codec.** Moving to better codecs on general-purpose
  servers was too costly, so YouTube built custom hardware. Google said the chip project started in 2015.
- **Viral videos invert the cache.** A video can go from zero to millions of views in an hour. The first wave of misses
  hits the origin together (the stampede problem, [lesson 3.6](#/lesson/3.6)). Request coalescing at the edge, and
  pushing popular content outward early, limit the damage.

## Try it

Design the upload-to-watch path for a 1 GB, 10-minute 1080p upload. Goals: a watchable version within 2 minutes of
upload finishing, full quality within 15 minutes, and no lost uploads on flaky mobile networks. List the steps, what
runs in parallel, and what the viewer sees at each stage.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

1. **Upload:** resumable, chunked (for example 8 MB chunks with offsets). Upload directly to blob storage with a
   signed URL, so app servers don't carry the bytes. On reconnect, resume from the last acknowledged chunk.
2. **On complete:** write the metadata row (status `processing`) and enqueue a job. Return immediately.
3. **Fast path (target under 2 min):** split the source into about 30 s chunks. Encode **360p and 720p H.264**
   in parallel on many workers (20 chunks x 2 renditions = 40 small jobs). Write the manifest and set status
   `watchable`. The viewer can now play.
4. **Full ladder (under 15 min):** queue 144p-1080p in H.264 plus VP9, and later AV1 only if views pass a threshold.
   Each finished rendition is added to the manifest, and players pick it up on their next manifest fetch.
5. **In parallel:** thumbnails, copyright and safety checks (publishing can wait on these), captions.
6. **Serve:** segments are fetched through the CDN/edge. A popular video is pushed to edges early. Misses are
   coalesced so one origin fetch serves many viewers.
7. **Failure handling:** each chunk job is idempotent (output path derived from video id + chunk + rendition), retried
   with backoff, with a dead-letter queue for poison inputs ([lesson 7.4](#/lesson/7.4)).
</details>
