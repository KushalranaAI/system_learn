# Sagas

A **saga** splits a cross-service transaction into a sequence of **local transactions**, one per service. If a later
step fails, earlier steps are undone by **compensating transactions**. There are no distributed locks; each step
commits on its own.

## Placing an order as a saga

| Step | Service | Action | Compensation |
|------|---------|--------|--------------|
| 1 | Orders | Create order (status `pending`) | Mark order `cancelled` |
| 2 | Inventory | Reserve items | Release the reservation |
| 3 | Payments | Authorise the card | Void the authorisation (or refund) |
| 4 | Orders | Mark order `confirmed` | n/a (the final step) |

If payment fails at step 3, the saga runs the compensations for steps 2 and 1: release stock, cancel the order.

```
create order ✓ → reserve stock ✓ → authorise payment ✗
                                     └─▶ release stock → cancel order
```

## Choreography vs orchestration

**Choreography:** services react to each other's events; no central brain.

```
OrderCreated → Inventory reserves → StockReserved → Payments authorises → PaymentAuthorised → Orders confirms
```

- ✅ Loosely coupled; easy for short flows.
- ❌ The flow is implicit, spread across services, and hard to see, change and debug as it grows.

**Orchestration:** a saga orchestrator (a service or workflow engine) tells each participant what to do and tracks
the state.

```
Orchestrator: CreateOrder → ReserveStock → AuthorisePayment → ConfirmOrder (or compensate in reverse)
```

- ✅ The flow is explicit, in one place, with timeouts and retries; easy to monitor.
- ❌ One more component; risk of putting too much logic in the orchestrator.

Workflow engines (Temporal, AWS Step Functions, Camunda) make orchestrated sagas durable: the saga's state survives
crashes and resumes where it left off.

## Designing good sagas

- **Every step and every compensation must be idempotent** (they'll be retried).
- **Compensations are business actions, not rollbacks:** a sent email can't be unsent; you send a correction. A
  captured payment is refunded, not erased.
- **Order steps by risk:** put steps most likely to fail (and cheapest to undo) first; irreversible steps (shipping,
  capturing money) last.
- **Use pending states** (`pending`, `reserved`, `authorised`) so other readers see that a saga is in flight.

## Isolation is the weak spot

Sagas lack isolation. Others can see intermediate states (stock reserved for an order that will be cancelled).
Countermeasures:
- **Semantic locks:** a `pending` flag tells others "in progress, treat with care".
- **Reread or verify** values before the final step.
- **Commutative updates** where possible (increments rather than overwrites).

## Try it

Design the saga for booking a trip: flight, hotel and car, all from different providers. The car is the most likely
to be unavailable. Order the steps and list the compensations.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Book the **car first** (most likely to fail, easy to cancel), then the hotel, then the flight (the most expensive to
cancel, often with fees), then charge the customer last. Compensations in reverse: cancel the flight → cancel the
hotel → cancel the car → release the payment hold. Each call carries an idempotency key; the orchestrator retries with
backoff, and if a compensation keeps failing, escalates to a human.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- A saga is a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions.
- Choreography suits short flows; orchestration (ideally with a workflow engine) suits longer ones.
- Steps and compensations must be idempotent; compensations are business actions.
- Put risky steps first and irreversible ones last; handle the lack of isolation with pending states.
