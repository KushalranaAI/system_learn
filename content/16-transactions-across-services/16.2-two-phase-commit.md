# Two-phase commit

The textbook way to make one transaction span several databases is **two-phase commit (2PC)**: a coordinator asks
every participant to prepare, and only commits if all say yes.

## The protocol

```
Phase 1 (prepare)
coordinator → orders DB:    "PREPARE tx 81"  → orders DB writes the change durably, locks rows, votes YES
coordinator → payments DB:  "PREPARE tx 81"  → votes YES

Phase 2 (commit)
all YES → coordinator logs "commit tx 81" → tells everyone "COMMIT" → they apply and release locks
any NO  → "ABORT" to everyone
```

Once a participant votes YES, it has **promised** it can commit, and it must keep its locks until it hears the
outcome.

## The blocking problem

If the coordinator crashes **after** participants voted YES but **before** sending the decision:
- participants can't commit (maybe someone voted NO);
- they can't abort (maybe the coordinator logged "commit" and told others);
- so they **hold their locks and wait**, possibly for a long time, blocking other transactions on those rows.

A coordinator outage becomes an outage of every participant's hot rows.

## Other costs

- **Latency:** two round trips to every participant, with locks held throughout.
- **Availability:** every participant must be up; one slow participant slows all.
- **Support:** participants must implement the protocol (XA). Many modern services, queues and HTTP APIs don't.

## Where 2PC is used

- **Inside** one distributed database: Spanner, CockroachDB and YugabyteDB use 2PC across their own shards, with the
  coordinator state replicated through consensus (Raft or Paxos), which removes the single-coordinator blocking
  problem.
- Some enterprise systems with XA-capable databases and message brokers.

## Across microservices

Rarely. Services own different technologies, deploy independently and must stay available on their own. Holding locks
across services while waiting on a coordinator couples them tightly. The usual answer is the **saga** (16.3): a
sequence of local transactions with compensations.

## Try it

A team proposes 2PC across the orders service (Postgres), the inventory service (MongoDB) and an external payment
provider's HTTP API. What's the problem?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The external payment API can't take part in 2PC (no prepare phase, no locks held for you), and the services would
block each other's rows during every checkout, coupling their availability. Use a **saga**: reserve stock → authorise
payment (with an idempotency key) → confirm the order, with compensations (release stock, void the authorisation) if
a later step fails.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- 2PC: prepare (vote) then commit or abort; participants hold locks in between.
- A coordinator crash after the votes blocks participants, holding locks.
- It adds latency, couples availability, and needs protocol support.
- It's used inside distributed databases (with replicated coordinators); across services, prefer sagas.
