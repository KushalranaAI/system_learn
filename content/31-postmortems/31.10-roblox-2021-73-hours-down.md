# Roblox, Oct 2021: 73 hours down

From 28 to 31 October 2021, Roblox, a game platform with about 50 million daily players, was down for **73 hours**.
Everything at Roblox found everything else through one HashiCorp **Consul** cluster. That cluster slowed to a crawl
under two problems at once: a new streaming feature with heavy lock contention, and a storage file whose internal
free list had grown out of control. The monitoring that would have shown this ran on Consul too.

## What users saw

- Roblox wouldn't load or let players join games. Player counts fell to half of normal three hours in, and the
  platform was soon fully down.
- Nothing came back for nearly three days. Then players returned in steps of about 10% on the last day.

## Timeline (Pacific time)

| Time | Event |
|------|-------|
| 27 Oct, 14:00 | Consul **streaming** enabled on a traffic-routing service; that service's node count raised by 50% |
| 28 Oct, 13:37 | Vault slows; one Consul server at high CPU. Players not yet affected |
| 28 Oct, afternoon | Suspect bad hardware; replace a Consul node; HashiCorp engineers join |
| 28 Oct, 16:35 | Online players down to 50% of normal; Roblox effectively down soon after |
| 28 Oct, evening | Consul moved to new 128-core machines (from 64). No improvement |
| 29 Oct, 02:00–04:00 | Return attempt 1: restore Consul snapshot from before the outage, limit load. Degrades again |
| 29 Oct, 16:00 – 30 Oct, 02:00 | Return attempt 2: cut non-essential Consul use, health checks every 10 min instead of 60 s. Degrades again |
| 30 Oct, 02:00–12:00 | Deep debugging: KV writes blocked; CPU time in kernel spin locks on the streaming code path |
| 30 Oct, 15:51 | Streaming disabled on all Consul systems; median KV write back to ~300 ms |
| 30 Oct, 20:00 – 31 Oct, 05:00 | Caching system redeployed from scratch |
| 31 Oct, 05:00–10:00 | Services restarted at full capacity |
| 31 Oct, 10:00–16:45 | Players let back in, ~10% at a time, via DNS steering |
| 31 Oct, 16:45 | 100% of players back |

## The trigger

Roblox had upgraded Consul and was gradually turning on its new **streaming** mechanism, which pushes updates to
clients instead of having them poll. On 27 October it went on for a busy traffic-routing service, which also got 50%
more nodes. Under Roblox's load, streaming caused heavy contention on a Go channel inside Consul, so writes blocked
behind reads.

## Why it spread

Consul wasn't one service among many. It was the foundation:

```
                         ┌──────────── Consul cluster (5 voters + 5 non-voters) ────────────┐
                         │  service discovery │ health checks │ KV store │ session locks    │
                         └─────────▲──────────────────▲───────────────▲───────────▲──────────┘
                                   │                  │               │           │
      every backend service ───────┘   Nomad (scheduling) ──┘   Vault (secrets) ──┘   telemetry ┘
                                                                                     (so: blind)
```

When Consul slowed, services couldn't find each other, Nomad couldn't schedule containers, and Vault couldn't hand out
secrets. 18,000 servers and 170,000 containers all depended on one cluster.

Two problems stacked:

1. **Streaming contention.** Turning it off on day 3 cut median KV write latency from about 2 s back to about 300 ms.
2. **BoltDB freelist growth.** Consul stores its Raft log in BoltDB. Freed pages go on a **freelist**, and the whole
   freelist is written out on every commit. The log store had grown to 4.2 GB holding only 489 MB of real data; the
   freelist tracked nearly a million free pages and had grown to 7.8 MB. So each small write (16 kB or less) also
   wrote 7.8 MB. That's why some Consul leaders stayed slow even after streaming was off.

And a **circular dependency**: Roblox's telemetry depended on Consul. When Consul was sick, the dashboards that would
have shown why were sick too.

## Why recovery took as long as it did

- **Wrong theory first.** High CPU on one node looked like bad hardware. Replacing hardware cost most of day 1, and the bigger
  128-core dual-socket machines actually made the lock contention worse.
- **No visibility.** With telemetry down, each hypothesis took hours to test.
- **Two causes.** Fixing one (streaming) didn't fully fix it, because the BoltDB problem remained. HashiCorp found the
  freelist cause in the days after the outage.
- **Cold start.** The caching system, serving about a billion requests per second, had to be redeployed from scratch,
  and its deployment tools were built for small changes to running clusters, not bootstrapping. Scheduling data in
  Consul was stale after the snapshot restore.
- **Thundering herd.** Letting 50 million players back at once would have knocked it over again, so they came back
  ~10% at a time over almost seven hours.

## What Roblox changed

- **Telemetry no longer depends on the systems it monitors.** New monitoring of Consul and BoltDB performance.
- **Split Consul** into multiple clusters for different workloads, instead of one cluster for everything.
- Streaming **disabled** until a new implementation is tested at Roblox scale.
- Replace BoltDB with bbolt (which fixes the freelist behaviour), and clean out obsolete KV data.
- **Redesign cache deployment** for fast bootstrap from nothing.
- Build out a geographically separate **second data centre** and move toward multiple availability zones.

## The general lesson

- **Remove circular dependencies, especially for monitoring.** The tools you use to debug an outage must not depend on
  what's broken. Same lesson as Facebook ([31.7](#/lesson/31.7)) and Cloudflare 2019 ([31.3](#/lesson/31.3)).
- **One shared control-plane cluster is one big blast radius.** Split it, or put services in cells that each have
  their own ([lesson 30.9](#/lesson/30.9)).
- **New features on critical infrastructure need gradual rollout and a fast off switch.** Streaming was rolled out
  over months, but the jump on a busy service wasn't tested at that load.
- **Storage engines have internal costs** that grow quietly: free lists, bloat, compaction ([lesson 28.5](#/lesson/28.5)).
- **Plan cold start and re-admit traffic gradually** ([lesson 3.6](#/lesson/3.6) on thundering herds).
- Consul's KV and leadership run on Raft ([lesson 26.7](#/lesson/26.7)), which is only as fast as its log writes.

## Try it

Remove a circular dependency. Your metrics, logs and alerting run on the same Kubernetes cluster as your app, and find
their storage through the same service registry. Sketch how to break the cycle without doubling your infrastructure.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

```
before:  app ─► registry ◄─ metrics/alerts       (registry down = app down AND blind)
after:   app ─► registry
         metrics/alerts ─► static IPs or their own small registry, separate cluster/account
```

- Run monitoring in a **separate, small cluster** (or a hosted provider), with its own DNS and auth.
- Point it at targets using **static config or cloud APIs**, not the production service registry.
- Keep a **minimal external probe** (from another provider) that checks your public endpoints and pages on failure.
- Make sure on-call can log in to monitoring without production SSO (break-glass account).
- Test it: in a game day, take down the registry and confirm dashboards and alerts still work.
</details>
