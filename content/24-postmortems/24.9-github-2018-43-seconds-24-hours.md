# GitHub, Oct 2018: 43 seconds, 24 hours

On 21 October 2018 the network link between GitHub's US East Coast hub and its main East Coast data centre dropped
for **43 seconds**. In that window, GitHub's automated failover promoted MySQL primaries on the **West Coast**. When
the link came back, both coasts had writes the other didn't. GitHub chose to protect data consistency over a quick
recovery, and ran degraded for **24 hours and 11 minutes**.

## What users saw

- github.com stayed up, but showed out-of-date and inconsistent data: missing recent comments, pushes that seemed to
  vanish, pull requests in odd states.
- Webhooks and GitHub Pages builds were paused for hours, then delivered with long delays.
- No user data was lost in the end, but some writes had to be reconciled by hand.

## The trigger

Routine maintenance to replace failing optical equipment. 43 seconds of lost connectivity is the kind of blip a
network has every so often. On its own it should have been a non-event.

## Why it spread

GitHub ran MySQL clusters with a primary on the East Coast and replicas on both coasts. **Orchestrator**, using
Raft consensus among nodes in several locations, decides when a primary is dead and promotes a replica.

```
          before                          during the 43 s                       after
 East (primary) ──► West (replica)   East ✕ hub   West promoted to primary   East has writes West never got
                                     Orchestrator quorum (West + cloud)      West has 30+ min of writes East never got
                                     sees East as dead                       ──► two diverged copies
```

1. The partition cut the East Coast data centre off from the rest of Orchestrator's quorum. The remaining nodes
   (West Coast and East Coast public cloud nodes) agreed the East Coast primaries were gone and promoted West Coast replicas.
2. A few seconds of writes on the East Coast primaries **hadn't replicated** west yet (asynchronous replication,
   [lesson 13.2](#/lesson/13.2)).
3. Applications started writing to the West Coast. When the link came back, the old East primaries had writes West
   didn't have, and West quickly had writes East didn't. You can't just point replication back east.
4. The apps ran in the East and now made every database write a cross-country round trip. Pages
   that make many sequential queries became very slow.

Orchestrator was configured to allow **cross-region promotion**. That's what turned a 43-second blip into a
split-brain ([lesson 5.4](#/lesson/5.4)).

## Why recovery took as long as it did

GitHub had a choice:

| Option | Speed | Cost |
|--------|-------|------|
| Fail back east right away | Fast | Throw away the West Coast writes made since 22:52 (or hand-merge them later) |
| Stay on West Coast | Fast | Cross-country latency for apps in the East; site barely usable |
| Restore East from backup, catch up from West, then fail back | **Many hours** | Keeps every write; degraded meanwhile |

They chose the third, saying the extended degradation was "worth ensuring the consistency of our users' data."

- Restoring **multiple terabytes** from backups took hours: transferring from the off-site backup service, then
  decompressing, checksumming, preparing and loading onto new MySQL servers.
- Then replicas had to catch up, while US traffic rose through the day.
- Then the queued webhooks and Pages builds had to drain. About 200,000 webhook payloads had outlived an internal TTL
  and were dropped.

The busiest affected cluster had 954 writes in the critical window that had to be reconciled.

## What GitHub changed

- Configure Orchestrator so it **cannot promote primaries across regions**.
- A status page that shows the state of **individual components**, not one global colour.
- Speed up the move to an **active/active/active** design across data centres, to survive losing a whole site.
- Invest in **fault injection and chaos engineering** to test assumptions like "failover is always safe."
- Better public estimates of time to repair.

## The general lesson

- **Failover is a decision, not a reflex.** A very short partition is often better ridden out than failed over.
  Promotion should wait long enough to be sure, and consider where the new primary is.
- **Async replication plus automatic failover means possible data divergence.** You must choose: lose a few seconds
  of writes and stay available, or stop and reconcile. Decide in advance ([lesson 13.6](#/lesson/13.6), CAP and
  PACELC).
- **Latency is part of availability.** A primary a continent away is "up" but may make the app unusable.
- **Test failover the way it will really happen**, including partial partitions ([lesson 18.6](#/lesson/18.6)).
- See [lesson 19.7](#/lesson/19.7) for how consensus decides leadership and why the minority side must step down.

## Try it

Decide between availability and consistency. Your East Coast primary has been unreachable for 30 seconds. The West
Coast replica is about 2 seconds behind. Your app servers are all on the East Coast. Do you promote the West Coast
replica? Write the rule your failover tool should follow.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

Usually **no, not automatically**:

- Promoting loses up to ~2 s of writes (or forces a reconciliation like GitHub's), and moves every DB call across the
  country, which may make the app unusable anyway.
- A 30-second partition is likely to heal on its own.

A reasonable rule:
- **Same-region** promotion: automatic after the primary is confirmed dead (e.g. 30 s, multiple observers).
- **Cross-region** promotion: only by a human, or automatically only if the whole region is confirmed lost and app
  traffic is also moving to that region.
- For data where losing writes is unacceptable (payments), use semi-synchronous replication to an in-region replica
  so promotion loses nothing.

The point is to choose before the incident: for each dataset, would you rather be briefly unavailable or briefly
inconsistent?
</details>
