# Facebook, Oct 2021: the internet forgot Facebook

On 4 October 2021 Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Messenger disappeared from the internet for about six hours.
The servers were running. A maintenance command cut every link in Facebook's backbone network, and Facebook's DNS
servers, built to step aside when they lose contact with the data centres, all stepped aside at once. The rest of the
internet could no longer even look up `facebook.com`. Engineers couldn't use their remote tools, and getting into the
data centres physically took time.

## What users saw

- Every Facebook-owned app failed to load. Browsers reported that `facebook.com` could not be resolved.
- Public DNS resolvers were flooded with retries: Cloudflare reported its 1.1.1.1 resolver handling about 30 times more
  queries than usual.
- "Log in with Facebook" broke on other sites, and businesses that ran on WhatsApp went silent.

## Timeline (UTC)

| Time | Event |
|------|-------|
| ~15:40 | Maintenance command takes down all backbone connections; a burst of BGP changes from Facebook |
| ~15:50 | `facebook.com` stops resolving on public resolvers |
| ~15:58 | Facebook's DNS prefixes are no longer announced anywhere (as seen by Cloudflare) |
| 15:58 onwards | Engineers can't reach the network remotely; out-of-band access is also down; teams sent to data centres |
| ~21:00 | BGP announcements from Facebook reappear |
| ~21:20 | `facebook.com` resolves again; services gradually restored |

Times are from outside observers (Cloudflare). Facebook's own posts give no clock times.

## The trigger

During routine maintenance, an engineer issued a command meant to **assess the availability of global backbone
capacity**. Instead it took down **all** connections in the backbone, the network that links Facebook's data centres
to each other and to its edge sites around the world. Commands like this go through an audit tool meant to catch
mistakes; a **bug in the audit tool** let it through.

## Why it spread

Facebook's authoritative DNS servers live in its edge sites. Each one advertises the DNS servers' IP addresses to the
internet with BGP ([lesson 20.5](#/lesson/20.5)), but only while it can reach the data centres. If it can't, it
withdraws the advertisement, so the internet's traffic goes to a healthier site. That's a sensible design for one
broken site.

```
command ──► backbone down ──► every edge site loses contact with every data centre
                                     │
                                     ▼  (health rule: "can't reach DCs → withdraw")
             every edge site withdraws its BGP route for Facebook's DNS servers
                                     │
                                     ▼
     the internet has no route to Facebook's DNS ──► facebook.com doesn't resolve
                                     │
                       ┌─────────────┼───────────────────────┐
                       ▼             ▼                       ▼
                 users: apps     internal tools that     remote / out-of-band
                 fail            use the same DNS fail   access down too
```

When **every** site is unhealthy, the same rule removes **every** route. A safety mechanism designed for a local
failure turned a backbone outage into a complete disappearance. And because Facebook's own internal tools used the
same DNS and network, the engineers lost their tools at the same moment users lost the apps. Reports at the time said
some staff badges stopped opening doors, as building access systems depended on the same network.

## Why recovery took as long as it did

- **No remote access.** Normal and out-of-band network access were both down. Engineers had to travel to data centres.
- **Physical security works.** The sites are built to be hard to get into and the hardware hard to modify. Getting
  the right people on site with the right access took time, by design.
- **Bringing load back carefully.** Power use had dropped by tens of megawatts per site. Turning all traffic back on at
  once risked electrical problems and overwhelmed caches, so services were restored gradually.
- Facebook had practised "storm" drills (taking out services, data centres and regions), which helped with the staged
  bring-up. It had never simulated losing the global backbone.

## What Facebook changed

Facebook's write-ups describe the cause and recovery, and say they will "strengthen our testing, drills, and overall
resilience", including finding ways to simulate a global backbone outage in future storm drills. They argued the security that slowed recovery was the right tradeoff day to day. The lessons others drew
are broader, and are in the next section.

## The general lesson

- **Health-based withdrawal needs a floor.** "Withdraw when unhealthy" is safe for one site and catastrophic for all.
  Keep a minimum: if every site would withdraw, the problem is probably not the sites. The same idea as AWS's NLB
  velocity limit in [lesson 24.5](#/lesson/24.5).
- **DNS is the front door.** Lose it and healthy servers are unreachable ([lesson 20.1](#/lesson/20.1)). Run
  authoritative DNS with a secondary provider or on independent infrastructure.
- **Out-of-band access must be truly out of band.** Separate network, separate DNS, separate authentication, and
  tested. If it shares a dependency with production, it isn't out of band.
- **Audit tools are code and have bugs.** A single check guarding a command that can disconnect everything is not
  enough; add a dry run, a scope limit and a staged application.
- **Recovery traffic is dangerous.** Bring load back in steps ([lesson 3.6](#/lesson/3.6) on cold caches and
  stampedes).

## Try it

Plan out-of-band access. Your company's production network and corporate network are both down because of a bad
router change. List what your on-call engineers need to fix it, and how each piece stays reachable.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

| Need | Independent path |
|------|------------------|
| Reach routers and switches | Console servers on a separate ISP or cellular modem, with their own IPs (no DNS needed) |
| Log in | Break-glass credentials that don't depend on the production SSO; stored offline and in a vault outside prod |
| Talk to each other | A chat or bridge line hosted by another provider; phone tree as last resort |
| Know what changed | Config history and runbooks mirrored outside the production network |
| Get into buildings | Badge system that works offline, plus a physical key process and a list of who can authorise it |
| Roll back | Routers keep the last known-good config; a "commit confirmed" that reverts automatically unless confirmed |

And drill it: once a quarter, pretend production DNS and SSO are gone and try to reach a router.
</details>
