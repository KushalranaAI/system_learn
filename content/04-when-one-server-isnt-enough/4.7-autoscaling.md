# Autoscaling

Traffic at 8 pm is 10 times traffic at 4 am. Paying for peak capacity all day is wasteful; running peak traffic on
night capacity is an outage. Autoscaling adds and removes servers as load changes.

## How it works

```
every 30–60 s:
  metric = average CPU across servers           (or requests per server, queue depth…)
  desired = ceil(current × metric ÷ target)     (target = 60% CPU)
  clamp desired to [min, max]; add or remove servers
```

With 4 servers at 90% CPU and a 60% target, desired = ceil(4 × 90 ÷ 60) = **6 servers**.

## Choosing the metric

| Metric | Good for | Watch out |
|--------|----------|-----------|
| **CPU utilisation** | CPU-bound APIs | Misses I/O-bound slowness |
| **Requests per server** | Predictable request cost | Needs a known per-server capacity |
| **Latency (p95/p99)** | User experience | Noisy; can scale on a slow *dependency* that more servers won't fix |
| **Queue depth / lag** | Background workers | Needs a per-worker throughput estimate |

## The warm-up problem

New servers aren't instant: boot the VM or container, start the app, warm the JIT and caches, pass readiness checks.
That's **1–5 minutes**.

```
traffic ─────────/‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾
capacity ────────────────/‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾
                 ▲       ▲
               spike    new servers ready (3 min later)
                 └───────┘ ← this gap is where you fail
```

Ways to close the gap:
- **Keep headroom:** target 50–60% utilisation, not 85%.
- **Scheduled scaling:** add capacity *before* known peaks (dinner time, a 9 am email blast, a sale).
- **Predictive scaling:** learn daily patterns and scale ahead.
- **Faster start-up:** small images, pre-warmed pools, lazy initialisation.
- **Shed load** while waiting: reject low-priority requests (429/503) instead of collapsing.

## Scaling in, safely

- Scale in **slowly** (e.g. remove one server per 5 minutes), and only after load has been low for a while.
  Otherwise the system flaps: add, remove, add, remove.
- **Drain** servers before terminating them (lesson 4.5).

## Limits

Autoscaling servers won't help when the bottleneck is elsewhere: a database at 100%, a rate-limited third-party API, a
lock. More servers can make it *worse* by opening more database connections. Always know what your next bottleneck
is.

## Try it

A food delivery app runs 4 servers at 40% CPU in the afternoon. At 7:30 pm traffic rises 3x within 5 minutes. New
servers take 4 minutes to become ready. The autoscaler targets 70% CPU. What happens, and what would you change?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

3x the traffic on 4 servers means 120% CPU demand, so they're overloaded within minutes, while new servers are still
4 minutes away. Fixes: scheduled scaling to ~10 servers at 7:15 pm, a lower target (50–60%) for more headroom, faster
images, and load shedding for non-critical endpoints during the gap.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- Autoscaling adjusts server count to a target metric within min/max bounds.
- New servers take minutes; headroom and scheduled scaling cover the gap.
- Scale in slowly and drain first.
- Autoscaling only helps when servers are the bottleneck.
