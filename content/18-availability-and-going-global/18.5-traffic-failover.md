# Traffic failover

When a region fails, users must be sent somewhere else: quickly, automatically, and without sending everyone back
the moment it flickers up again.

## Three mechanisms

| Mechanism | How it moves traffic | Speed | Notes |
|-----------|---------------------|-------|-------|
| **DNS failover** | Health-checked DNS returns the healthy region's IPs | Minutes (TTLs + resolver caching that ignores TTLs) | Simple and cheap; slow and imprecise |
| **Anycast** | The same IP is announced from many locations; routing sends users to the nearest healthy one | Seconds | How CDNs and global load balancers work |
| **Global load balancer** | One global entry point (often anycast) routes to regional backends by health, latency and capacity | Seconds | Cloud global LBs, CDN load balancing |

## DNS details

- Use **low TTLs** (30–60 s) on records you may need to change, but many resolvers and clients cache longer.
- **Health checks** must test the real path (an endpoint exercising app + DB), from several locations.
- Latency-based or geo DNS also gets users to their nearest region in normal times.

## Avoiding flapping

A region that's half-broken (lesson 12.1) can bounce between healthy and unhealthy, dragging users back and forth.
- Require N consecutive failures to fail over, and N successes to fail back.
- Make **failback manual**, or slow and gradual, after a real outage.
- Shift traffic **gradually** (10% → 50% → 100%) when moving back, to avoid a cold-cache stampede.

## Capacity and dependencies

- The surviving region must have the **capacity** (lesson 18.4) and **warm caches**. A failover into a cold, small
  region can just move the outage.
- Data must be ready: in active-passive, the database must be promoted *before* traffic arrives.
- Global dependencies (auth, DNS, the control plane) must not live only in the failed region.

## Game days

Untested failover doesn't work when needed. Rehearse it:
- fail over a region on purpose, on a schedule, during business hours;
- measure detection time, the traffic shift, error rates and data loss;
- update the runbook with every surprise.

## Try it

DNS failover with a 300 s TTL takes ~12 minutes to move most users during a test, and some mobile clients take hours.
What would you change?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Lower the TTL (30–60 s) on the failover records. Better, put an **anycast global load balancer / CDN** in front, so the
public IP never changes and routing to backends switches in seconds. Fix clients that pin IPs or cache DNS forever.
Keep the health-check thresholds and gradual failback.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- DNS failover is simple but slow; anycast and global LBs move traffic in seconds.
- Health-check the real path; require consecutive failures; fail back slowly.
- The target region needs capacity, warm caches and a promoted database first.
- Rehearse failovers with game days.
