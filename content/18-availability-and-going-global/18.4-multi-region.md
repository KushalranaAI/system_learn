# Multi-region

Going multi-region protects against a whole region failing and brings the service closer to users worldwide. It's
also the most complex step in this course. The hard part is **data**, not servers.

## Active-passive (warm standby)

```
users ─▶ Region A (active: serves all traffic) ──async replication──▶ Region B (standby: replicas, scaled-down servers)
on disaster: promote B's database, scale up B, switch DNS / global LB to B
```

- ✅ Simple data story: one primary region, so no write conflicts.
- ✅ Cheaper (the standby can be smaller).
- ❌ Failover takes minutes (promote, scale, switch traffic). Practise it.
- ❌ **Data loss = replication lag** at the moment of failure (RPO of seconds), since replication is async.
- ❌ Users far from A still pay the latency.

## Active-active

```
users (Asia) ─▶ Region A ⇄ data sync ⇄ Region B ◀─ users (Europe)
both regions serve reads and writes
```

- ✅ Low latency for everyone; a region failure just shifts traffic (seconds).
- ❌ **Writes in two places** means conflicts (lesson 13.4) or a global consensus database (Spanner, CockroachDB)
  paying cross-region latency on writes.
- ❌ More expensive (full capacity in each region, plus headroom to absorb the other region's traffic).

## Patterns that make active-active workable

| Pattern | How |
|---------|-----|
| **Home region per user/tenant** | Each user's writes go to their home region; other regions read replicas. Conflicts avoided by design |
| **Global data in a consensus DB** | Small, critical data (usernames, account balances) in a multi-region linearizable store; the rest is local |
| **CRDTs / mergeable data** | Counters, sets, collaborative docs (lesson 13.7) |
| **Region-local data** | Caches, sessions, rate-limit counters: no need to replicate |

## Data residency

Laws (GDPR, India's DPDP Act, sector rules) may require some data to **stay in a country or region**. Homing users in
their region helps; global analytics must use anonymised or aggregated copies.

## Capacity

If 2 active regions each run at 70%, and one fails, the other must take 140%: **it falls over**. Active-active needs
each region sized to absorb the others' load (e.g. 2 regions at ≤ 50%, or 3 at ≤ 66%).

## Try it

A payments startup serves India only and wants to survive a full region outage with at most 1 minute of lost data and
30 minutes of downtime. Active-passive or active-active?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Active-passive** across two Indian regions (which also keeps data in-country). Async replication keeps the RPO at
seconds (well under 1 minute); an automated, rehearsed failover runbook meets a 30-minute RTO. Active-active adds
write-conflict complexity that isn't needed for one country's latency.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- Active-passive: simple data, minutes of failover, RPO = replication lag.
- Active-active: low latency and fast failover, but write conflicts or global consensus.
- Home users in a region, keep critical global data in a consensus store, keep ephemeral data local.
- Size each region to absorb the others; mind data residency.
