# SLIs, SLOs and SLAs

"Make it highly available" isn't a requirement. "99.9% of checkout requests succeed within 500 ms, measured over 28
days" is. Three terms turn reliability into something you can measure and manage.

## The three terms

| Term | What it is | Example |
|------|------------|---------|
| **SLI**, service level indicator | A measured ratio of good events to all events | Successful checkout requests under 500 ms ÷ all checkout requests |
| **SLO**, service level objective | Your target for the SLI over a window | 99.9% over 28 days |
| **SLA**, service level agreement | A contract with customers, with penalties | 99.5% monthly, or service credits |

The SLA is looser than the SLO, so you have a margin before you owe anyone money.

## Good SLIs

Measure what **users** experience, as close to them as practical:

| Service type | SLI |
|--------------|-----|
| Request/response API | Availability (non-5xx share), latency (share under a threshold) |
| Data pipeline | Freshness (data less than N minutes old), correctness |
| Storage | Durability, successful reads/writes |
| Streaming/live | Share of events delivered within N seconds |

Prefer "the share of requests faster than 300 ms" over "average latency" (lesson 2.7).

## Error budgets

A 99.9% SLO means **0.1% of requests may fail**. That's your **error budget**.

| SLO | Allowed downtime per 30 days |
|-----|------------------------------|
| 99% | 7.2 hours |
| 99.5% | 3.6 hours |
| 99.9% | 43 minutes |
| 99.95% | 22 minutes |
| 99.99% | 4.3 minutes |

How teams use it:
- Budget left: ship features, run experiments, take some risk.
- Budget burning fast: slow down releases, prioritise reliability work.
- **Alert on burn rate** ("we'll exhaust the month's budget in 2 days at this rate"), not on every blip.

The simulator's incident summary reports **error budget used**: the share of a month's 99.9% budget one incident
burned. One bad failover can eat a week's worth.

## Choosing the target

- **100% is the wrong target.** It's unachievable, and chasing it freezes all change.
- Each extra nine costs roughly 10x more effort (lesson 18.2).
- Set it from what users notice and what the business needs. Internal tools rarely need more than 99.5%; payments
  might need 99.99%.
- Users depend on a chain: your SLO can't exceed what your dependencies give you, unless you design around them.

## Try it

A checkout API handled 40M requests in 28 days, and 52,000 failed (5xx or over 1 s). The SLO is 99.9%. Is it met?
How much budget remains?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The budget is 0.1% of 40M = **40,000 failures**. 52,000 failed, so the SLO is **missed** by 12,000 (actual
availability 99.87%). Reliability work takes priority over risky launches until it recovers.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- SLI = what you measure, SLO = your target, SLA = the contract (looser than the SLO).
- Measure user-visible good/total ratios: availability, latency thresholds, freshness.
- The error budget (1 − SLO) guides when to ship and when to stabilise; alert on burn rate.
- Choose targets deliberately; every nine costs much more.
