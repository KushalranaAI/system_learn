# The cost of nines

Availability compounds. Chain components and it **drops**; duplicate them and it **rises**. A little arithmetic tells
you where redundancy actually matters.

## In series: multiply

A request needs **all** of them:

```
LB 99.99% × API 99.9% × DB 99.9% × payment provider 99.9%
= 0.9999 × 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 ≈ 99.69%   (~2.2 hours of downtime a month)
```

Each dependency in the path lowers the ceiling. **Ten** 99.9% services in series give about **99.0%**.

## In parallel: multiply the failures

A request needs **any one** of N redundant copies:

```
availability = 1 − (1 − a)^N
two 99% servers:   1 − 0.01²  = 99.99%
three 99% servers: 1 − 0.01³  = 99.9999%
```

This assumes failures are **independent**. Two servers in the same rack, on the same power, running the same buggy
deploy, are not independent. That's why redundancy spans **zones and regions**, and why deploys roll out gradually.

## Detection and failover take time

Redundancy only helps once traffic moves. If a failure takes 2 minutes to detect and fail over, and happens monthly,
you've spent 2 minutes of a 4.3-minute (99.99%) budget on one event. **Fast detection and automatic failover are part
of availability**, not extras. The simulator models this: failover takes 15 s, and you can see it on the chart.

## Where the nines go

| Target | Typically requires |
|--------|-------------------|
| 99% | One server, decent monitoring, manual recovery |
| 99.9% | Redundant servers behind an LB, a DB replica with failover, alerting, safe deploys |
| 99.99% | Multi-zone everything, automatic failover in seconds, no single points of failure, gradual rollouts, practised incident response |
| 99.999% | Multi-region active-active, cell architecture, extreme change discipline, and a lot of money |

## Planned downtime counts

Maintenance windows, migrations and deploys all spend budget unless they're zero-downtime: rolling deploys, online
schema migrations, draining (lesson 4.5), blue-green releases.

## Mean time to recovery matters most

```
availability ≈ MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR)
```

You can raise availability by failing less often (MTBF, mean time between failures) **or by recovering faster**
(MTTR, mean time to recovery). Cutting MTTR from 60 to 6 minutes is often cheaper than preventing failures, through
automated failover, quick rollbacks, feature flags and good runbooks.

## Try it

Checkout depends in series on: CDN (99.99%), LB (99.99%), API (2 servers, each 99.5%, behind the LB), DB (99.95%) and
a payment provider (99.9%). Estimate overall availability. Which component dominates?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

API pair: 1 − 0.005² = 99.9975%. Overall ≈ 0.9999 × 0.9999 × 0.999975 × 0.9995 × 0.999 ≈ **99.83%**. The **payment
provider** (99.9%) and the **DB** (99.95%) dominate. Improve them with a second payment provider (failover) and a
faster DB failover.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- In series, availabilities multiply and every dependency lowers the ceiling.
- In parallel, failure probabilities multiply, if failures are independent.
- Detection and failover time eat into the budget; automate them.
- Faster recovery (MTTR) is often the cheapest way to more nines.
