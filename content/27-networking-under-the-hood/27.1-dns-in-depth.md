# DNS in depth

Almost every request starts with a DNS lookup, and most of the time it costs nothing because the answer is cached.
This lesson opens up the lookup: who asks whom, what gets cached and for how long, and why a DNS mistake tends to
take down everything at once rather than one server.

## The cast

| Role | What it does | Example |
|------|--------------|---------|
| **Stub resolver** | Library in your OS or runtime. Asks one server and waits. | `getaddrinfo()` |
| **Recursive resolver** | Does the legwork: walks the hierarchy, caches every answer. | Your ISP, 8.8.8.8, 1.1.1.1, the VPC resolver |
| **Root servers** | Know who runs each top-level domain. 13 names (a–m), each served from many anycast sites. | `a.root-servers.net` |
| **TLD servers** | Know which name servers are authoritative for each domain. | `.com` servers |
| **Authoritative server** | Holds the actual records for a zone. | Route 53, Cloudflare DNS, your own BIND |

## A cold lookup, traced

A user's laptop resolves `api.shop.example` with an empty resolver cache:

```
laptop  → resolver   : A? api.shop.example                 (UDP :53, 1 packet)
resolver → root      : A? api.shop.example
root     → resolver  : "ask .example servers"  NS + glue IPs, TTL 2 days
resolver → .example  : A? api.shop.example
.example → resolver  : "ask ns1.dnsprovider.net"  NS, TTL 2 days
resolver → ns1       : A? api.shop.example
ns1      → resolver  : api.shop.example A 203.0.113.10  TTL 60
resolver → laptop    : 203.0.113.10 TTL 60
```

Four round trips for the resolver, maybe 80–200 ms in total. The next user of the same resolver gets the answer from
cache in under a millisecond, and the root and TLD answers stay cached for days, so in practice a resolver mostly
talks to authoritative servers.

What's in the packet: a 12-byte header (a 16-bit query ID, flags, counts), the question, then answer, authority and
additional sections. Classic DNS over UDP was limited to 512 bytes; EDNS0 lets a client advertise a bigger buffer. If
an answer still doesn't fit, the server sets the **TC** (truncated) bit and the client retries over TCP. Firewalls
that block TCP/53 break large answers (DNSSEC, many TXT records) in confusing ways.

## TTLs: every answer has an expiry

Every record carries a **TTL** in seconds. A resolver may reuse the answer until it expires.

| TTL | Good for | Cost |
|-----|----------|------|
| 1–2 days | NS records, rarely changing apex records | A change takes days to reach everyone |
| 1 hour | Stable service records | Failover takes up to an hour for cached clients |
| 30–60 s | Records you may fail over | More queries to your authoritative servers |
| 0–5 s | Rarely sensible | Resolvers may clamp it; every request pays for a lookup |

Two traps:

- **TTL changes are not instant.** If a record had TTL 86400 and you lower it to 60, resolvers that cached it
  yesterday keep the old copy for up to a day. Lower the TTL *at least one old TTL before* a planned migration.
- **Not everyone honours TTLs.** Some resolvers enforce a minimum, some runtimes and connection pools cache an
  address for the life of the process, and long-lived connections never look up again at all. Failover via DNS is
  only as fast as the slowest cache in the path.

## Negative caching

"This name does not exist" (NXDOMAIN) is cached too. The TTL for a negative answer comes from the zone's SOA record
(the smaller of the SOA TTL and its minimum field, per RFC 2308). If you query `new-api.shop.example` before creating
it and the negative TTL is 1 hour, resolvers that saw the NXDOMAIN keep saying "no such name" for up to an hour after
you add the record. Create records before anything starts asking for them.

## Why DNS outages are global

A bug in one web server takes out that server. A bad DNS answer is different:

1. **It is served everywhere.** Authoritative servers are replicated and anycast (lesson 27.5), so a wrong record is
   faithfully replicated to every site within seconds.
2. **It gets cached.** Resolvers keep the bad answer, and the negative answer, for its TTL even after you fix it.
3. **Everything depends on it.** Service discovery, internal APIs, health checks, even your own tooling and status
   page often resolve names through the same system.

Real examples:

- **AWS us-east-1, October 2025** ([lesson 31.5](#/lesson/31.5)): a race condition between two instances of
  DynamoDB's automated DNS management left `dynamodb.us-east-1.amazonaws.com` with an empty record at 11:48 PM PDT.
  Every client, including many AWS services, suddenly could not find DynamoDB. DNS was restored at 2:25 AM, but the
  knock-on effects (EC2 launches, load balancer health checks) lasted until the afternoon.
  [Open it in the sandbox](#/sandbox?preset=out-aws-dns-2025).
- **Facebook, October 2021** ([lesson 31.7](#/lesson/31.7)): the DNS servers were healthy but withdrew their own
  routes, so nobody could reach them.
- **Dyn, October 2016**: a large DDoS against one DNS provider made many big sites that relied on it alone
  unreachable for hours.

Defences: two independent DNS providers for critical zones, careful automation with checks that refuse to publish an
empty or drastically smaller record set, staged changes, and generous TTLs on records you don't plan to move.

## Inside your own network

Service meshes and Kubernetes lean on DNS heavily. A pod looking up `orders` may try `orders.default.svc.cluster.local`,
`orders.svc.cluster.local` and more (the `ndots` search list), multiplying queries by four or five. At high request
rates the cluster DNS becomes a hot spot. Cache locally (node-local DNS cache), use fully qualified names with a
trailing dot, and reuse connections so you don't resolve on every call.

## Try it

1. Trace the lookup of `img.cdn.example` from a cold resolver: which servers are asked, and what does each return?
2. You run an active/passive pair: `app.example` points to region A, and on failure you'll point it to region B. Pick
   the TTL for `app.example`, and say what else you need for failover to really work.

1. Resolver → a root server: returns NS records (plus glue IPs) for `.example`. Resolver → a `.example` TLD server:
   returns the NS records for `cdn.example`. Resolver → the authoritative server for `cdn.example`: returns the A/AAAA
   record (or a CNAME it then follows). The resolver caches all three answers by their TTLs and hands the final
   address to the stub resolver.
2. A TTL of about **60 seconds** is the usual choice: fast enough that most clients move within a minute or two, without
   hammering the authoritative servers. Also: health checks that flip the record automatically (Route 53 failover
   records or similar), a lower TTL set well in advance if the record used to be long-lived, clients that don't pin
   addresses forever (connection pools with a max lifetime), region B warmed up and able to take full load, and a
   rehearsed runbook. Expect a tail of clients that take longer than the TTL.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- Stub → recursive resolver → root → TLD → authoritative; caching makes nearly all lookups cheap.
- TTLs bound how fast changes spread; lower them well before a planned move.
- NXDOMAIN is cached too, via the SOA's negative TTL.
- A bad DNS answer is replicated and cached everywhere, which is why DNS failures look global.
