# TLS and certificates

TLS gives a connection three things: **encryption** (nobody on the path can read it), **integrity** (nobody can
change it unnoticed) and **authentication** (you're talking to the real `shop.example`). The first two come from the
handshake's key exchange. The third comes from certificates, and certificates are what cause outages.

## The TLS 1.3 handshake

TLS 1.2 needed two round trips before application data. TLS 1.3 needs one, because the client guesses the key
exchange group and sends its key share up front.

```
client                                              server
  | ClientHello: versions, cipher suites,              |
  |   key_share (X25519 public key), SNI=shop.example  |
  |--------------------------------------------------->|
  |   ServerHello: chosen suite, key_share             |
  |   --- from here on, everything is encrypted ---    |
  |   {EncryptedExtensions}                            |
  |   {Certificate: leaf + intermediates}              |
  |   {CertificateVerify: signature over handshake}    |
  |   {Finished}                                       |
  |<---------------------------------------------------|
  | {Finished} + first HTTP request                    |
  |--------------------------------------------------->|
```

- Both sides combine their key shares (Diffie–Hellman) into a shared secret. Because the keys are ephemeral, a
  stolen server private key later can't decrypt recorded traffic (**forward secrecy**).
- `CertificateVerify` proves the server holds the private key matching the certificate.
- **SNI** (server name) in the ClientHello tells a shared IP which certificate to present. It's sent in clear text
  unless Encrypted Client Hello is used.

On top of TCP that's 1 RTT for TCP plus 1 RTT for TLS before the request goes out. QUIC merges the two (lesson 27.4).

## Session resumption and 0-RTT

After a full handshake, the server can hand the client a **session ticket** (a pre-shared key). Next time:

- **Resumption (1-RTT):** skip certificate transfer and verification; cheaper CPU, smaller packets.
- **0-RTT (early data):** the client sends its request *with* the ClientHello. Zero extra round trips, but an
  attacker who records that first flight can **replay** it. Only allow 0-RTT for idempotent requests (GETs), never
  for "place order".

Behind a load balancer, every server must be able to decrypt tickets issued by any other, so ticket-encryption keys
are shared across the fleet and rotated regularly.

## Certificate chains

```
[ Root CA ]           in the client's trust store (OS / browser), lives 20+ years
     | signs
[ Intermediate CA ]   sent by the server, lives a few years
     | signs
[ Leaf: shop.example ] sent by the server, lives days to months
```

The client validates: each signature in the chain, that it ends at a trusted root, that every certificate is within
its validity dates, that the name matches (SAN list, with wildcards covering one label), and that it isn't revoked.

Common failures:

| Symptom | Cause |
|---------|-------|
| Works in Chrome, fails in curl or on Android | Server forgot to send the intermediate; browsers sometimes fetch or cache it, other clients don't |
| Fails only on old devices | Root missing from their trust store, or a root has expired |
| Fails for everyone at the same moment | **The leaf or an intermediate expired** |
| Fails for one hostname | Name not in the SAN list |

## Expiry outages

Expiry is a time bomb with a known date, and it still goes off regularly:

- **Ericsson / O2, December 2018**: an expired certificate in Ericsson's packet-core software knocked out data for
  O2's roughly 32 million UK customers and hit operators in other countries for most of a day.
- **Microsoft Teams, February 2020**: an expired authentication certificate stopped users from signing in for hours.
- **Let's Encrypt root, September 2021**: the old DST Root CA X3 expired. Up-to-date clients used the newer ISRG root
  and were fine, but older devices and libraries that couldn't build the new chain started failing.

The pattern: the certificate was renewed manually once a year or every few years, the person who knew moved on, and
there was no monitoring of the expiry date from the outside. Internal certificates (service-to-service mTLS,
databases, message brokers, appliances) are the usual culprit because they're invisible to public monitoring.

## Lifetimes are getting shorter

The CA/Browser Forum's ballot SC-081 caps the lifetime of public TLS certificates at **200 days** for certificates
issued from March 15, 2026, **100 days** from March 2027 and **47 days** from March 2029. Let's Encrypt is moving
from 90-day to 45-day certificates by 2028. At 47 days, manual renewal is no longer an option; it must be automated
(ACME), and the automation itself must be monitored.

## Try it

Plan certificate rotation for **500 domains**: a mix of public sites behind your CDN and load balancers, plus internal
service certificates. Assume 47-day maximum lifetimes.

<details>
<summary>Reference</summary>

- **Inventory first.** One source of truth for every certificate: hostname, where it's deployed (CDN, LB, pods,
  appliances), issuer, expiry, owner. Discover unknown ones by scanning your IP ranges and Certificate Transparency
  logs for your domains.
- **Automate issuance with ACME.** Public: a managed CDN/LB certificate service or cert-manager / an ACME client.
  Internal: a private CA (with its own short-lived certs) so you don't depend on public CAs or leak internal names to
  CT logs.
- **Renew early.** Renew at about two-thirds of the lifetime (around day 30 of 47), so a failing renewal has two weeks
  of retries before anything breaks.
- **Spread the load.** 500 certificates / 30 days ≈ 17 renewals a day; stagger them rather than renewing all at once,
  and respect CA rate limits. Group related names with SANs or wildcards where it reduces count without widening
  blast radius too much.
- **Deploy without restarts.** Servers reload certificates on file change or fetch from a secret store; roll out
  gradually and check the new cert is actually served.
- **Monitor from the outside.** A probe connects to every endpoint daily and alerts on certificates under 14 days, on
  renewal failures, and on chain or name errors. Alert to the owning team, not a shared inbox.
- **Keep a manual runbook** for the CA itself being down, and a second CA configured as a fallback.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- TLS 1.3: one round trip, forward secrecy by default; 0-RTT is fast but replayable.
- Clients validate the chain, dates, name and revocation; servers must send intermediates.
- Expired certificates cause sudden, total outages at a predictable moment.
- With lifetimes falling toward 47 days, automate renewal and monitor every endpoint from outside.
