# Common mistakes

These show up again and again in design reviews and interviews. Each one has a simple antidote.

## 1. Designing before understanding
Jumping to "Kafka + Cassandra + Kubernetes" before asking how many users there are.
**Antidote:** requirements and estimates first (lesson 10.1). Write the numbers down.

## 2. Over-engineering
Microservices, multi-region active-active and sharding for 200 requests a second.
**Antidote:** the simplest design that meets the numbers, plus a stated plan for growth. One server and one database
is a valid answer for many problems.

## 3. Ignoring the write path
A beautiful cached read path, while writes, which can't be cached, overwhelm the primary.
**Antidote:** estimate reads and writes separately; draw both paths.

## 4. Single points of failure
One load balancer, one cache, one database, and no plan for any of them dying.
**Antidote:** for each component ask "what if it dies?" Add redundancy where an outage isn't acceptable.

## 5. Forgetting failure modes
Assuming every call succeeds quickly: no timeouts, no retries, or retries without backoff.
**Antidote:** timeouts everywhere, idempotent retries with backoff and jitter, and circuit breakers (Module 12).

## 6. Averages instead of percentiles
"Average latency 40 ms" while p99 is 2 s.
**Antidote:** targets and measurements at p95/p99 (lesson 2.7).

## 7. Running hot
Planning servers or databases at 90%+ utilisation "to save money".
**Antidote:** queueing math (lesson 4.1). Target 50–70% and plan for N+1.

## 8. Caches without a story
"Add a cache" with no key design, TTL, invalidation or cold-start plan.
**Antidote:** pattern, key, TTL, invalidation, stampede protection (Module 3).

## 9. Hand-waving consistency
"It's eventually consistent" without saying what users see, or for how long.
**Antidote:** say per feature what staleness is acceptable and how you bound it (read-your-writes, versioning).

## 10. Unbounded things
Unbounded queues, unpaginated lists, unlimited retries, unbounded fan-out.
**Antidote:** every list paginated, every queue bounded and monitored, every retry capped.

## 11. Exactly-once magical thinking
"The queue guarantees exactly-once, so we won't double-charge."
**Antidote:** at-least-once plus idempotency keys (lesson 7.3).

## 12. Not naming tradeoffs
Presenting a design as if it has no downsides.
**Antidote:** need → choice → why → **cost** (lesson 10.2).

## Try it

Review this design: *"A ticket-booking site for concerts. Users hit a single Node server that holds seat availability
in memory. When a seat is booked we charge the card, then send the confirmation email, then write the booking to
MongoDB. We expect 1M users when big concerts open."* List at least five mistakes.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

1. A single server holding state in memory: no redundancy, can't scale out, and the state is lost on restart.
2. No estimates for the on-sale spike (1M users at once).
3. The card is charged **before** the booking is durably recorded: a crash loses the booking but keeps the money.
4. The email is sent synchronously in the request.
5. No double-booking protection under concurrency (locking or conditional writes).
6. No idempotency on the charge (retries double-charge).
7. No queue or waiting room to absorb the on-sale spike.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- Most mistakes come from skipping requirements, numbers or failure analysis.
- Prefer the simplest design that meets the numbers, plus a growth plan.
- Bound everything; make retries idempotent; target percentiles and headroom.
- Always name the tradeoffs.
