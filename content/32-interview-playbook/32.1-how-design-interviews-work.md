# How design interviews work

A system design interview is not a quiz with one right answer. It's a 45-60 minute working session where the
interviewer watches **how you think**: how you narrow a vague prompt, how you use numbers, how you weigh tradeoffs and
how you react when they push back. This module gives you a repeatable plan and 16 of the most-asked problems, each
worked through the same way.

## What interviewers score

Every company writes its own rubric, but they cluster around the same four themes (public prep guides such as Hello
Interview describe almost exactly these):

| Theme | What it looks like when it goes well | What it looks like when it goes badly |
|-------|-------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Problem navigation** | You turn "design X" into a short list of features and numbers, and spend time on the parts that matter | You start drawing boxes in minute two, or wander through 10 topics shallowly |
| **Solution design** | A working end-to-end design, then improvements where the numbers demand them | Fancy parts with no reason, or a design that doesn't actually serve the main request |
| **Technical excellence** | You can go two or three levels down on the hard part (e.g. "how does the hold expire?") | Buzzwords ("we'll use Kafka") with no idea what the part does |
| **Communication and collaboration** | You narrate, state assumptions, invite questions and handle "what if" calmly | Long silences, or talking over the interviewer, or defending a choice you can't justify |

**Level matters.** For a mid-level role, a complete, sensible design with one solid deep dive is a pass. For senior
and staff roles, the interviewer expects you to **drive**: pick the deep dives yourself, raise failure modes before
being asked, and talk about operations, cost and evolution.

The single most common way to fail is simple: **running out of time without a working design.** A plain design that
works beats a brilliant half of one.

## A 45-minute plan

This is the framework from [lesson 10.1](#/lesson/10.1), squeezed into a real interview slot (most loops give you
about 45 minutes after introductions).

| Minutes | Step | What you produce |
|---------|------|------------------|
| 0-5 | **Clarify and requirements** | 3 core features, 3-5 non-functional goals with numbers, what's out of scope |
| 5-9 | **Estimates** | Peak QPS per path, read:write ratio, storage, anything that changes the design |
| 9-13 | **API and data model** | 3-6 endpoints; the main entities, their keys and the store for each |
| 13-25 | **High-level design** | Boxes and arrows; walk each endpoint through them, write path and read path separately |
| 25-40 | **Deep dives** | The 2-3 hardest parts for *this* problem, in depth |
| 40-45 | **Wrap up** | Bottlenecks, failure modes, what you'd do at 10x, questions |

Two adjustments that interviewers appreciate:

- **Do estimates only where they change a decision.** "100k redirects/s means a cache is mandatory" is useful.
  Computing bandwidth to three decimal places is not. Say which numbers you're skipping and why.
- **Build simple first, then layer.** Draw the version with one server and one database, check that it serves every
  endpoint, then add caches, queues and shards and say what number made you add each one.

## Driving the conversation

- **Think out loud.** The interviewer can only score what they hear. "I'm choosing between fan-out on write and on
  read; writes are 1% of traffic, so..." is worth more than the right answer in silence.
- **State assumptions and move on.** "I'll assume 100M daily users; tell me if that's off." Don't wait for permission
  for every number.
- **Offer choices at the forks.** "I can go deeper on key generation or on caching the redirects. Which is more
  interesting to you?" This shows you know where the hard parts are, and lets the interviewer steer to their rubric.
- **Treat pushback as a hint, not an attack.** "What if the cache dies?" usually means "you haven't covered this."
  Answer it directly, adjust the design, and keep going.
- **Name the tradeoff in every decision.** Every choice gives something up ([lesson 1.5](#/lesson/1.5)). Saying what
  you lose is a strong signal.
- **Keep the diagram readable.** Label arrows with what flows and which way. Redraw rather than squeeze.
- **Watch the clock.** If you're at minute 25 still on the API, say so and jump to the high-level design.

## The shape of every problem lesson

Each of 25.2-25.17 follows the same layout so you can practise the rhythm:

1. Clarifying questions you'd ask.
2. Requirements: functional and non-functional.
3. Back-of-the-envelope estimates with the arithmetic shown ([lesson 1.4](#/lesson/1.4)).
4. API.
5. Data model.
6. High-level design (a diagram).
7. Two or three deep dives.
8. Bottlenecks and failure modes.
9. What interviewers listen for, and common mistakes.
10. A "Try it" exercise, most with a sandbox starting point.

Read the lesson, close it, then do the problem on paper against a 45-minute timer. Compare afterwards. The
[common mistakes lesson](#/lesson/10.3) and [explaining your design](#/lesson/10.2) are worth rereading before a loop.

## Useful numbers to have ready

| Number | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Seconds in a day | ~86,400 (round to 100k for quick maths) |
| Seconds in a month | ~2.6M |
| 1M requests/day | ~12/s average |
| Peak vs average | 2-5x for most consumer apps; much more for events and flash sales |
| One Postgres/MySQL primary | a few thousand writes/s, tens of thousands of simple reads/s |
| One Redis node | ~100k simple ops/s |
| One WebSocket server | hundreds of thousands of idle connections with tuning ([lesson 6.2](#/lesson/6.2)) |
| Same-region round trip | ~0.5-1 ms; cross-continent ~100-150 ms |

## Try it

Run a mock interview on yourself. Set a 45-minute timer, pick **"Design a pastebin"**, and follow the plan above,
saying everything out loud (record yourself if you can). Afterwards, score yourself 1-4 on each of the four themes.

- **0-5:** create a paste, read a paste by short id, optional expiry; out of scope: accounts, editing. 10M new

## Key takeaways

- Interviewers score problem navigation, solution design, technical excellence, and communication and collaboration.
- Deliver a working end-to-end design first; failing to finish is the most common reason people fail.
- Spend the most time on the 2-3 parts that are hard for this problem, and say why you chose them.
- Think out loud, state assumptions, name the tradeoff in every decision.
