# Backups and recovery

Replicas protect you from a dead machine. They don't protect you from `DELETE FROM orders;` without a `WHERE`,
because replicas faithfully copy that delete within milliseconds. **Backups protect you from mistakes, bugs, attackers
and disasters.**

## Two numbers define your plan

| | Question | Example target |
|-|----------|----------------|
| **RPO**, recovery point objective | How much data can we lose? | 5 minutes |
| **RTO**, recovery time objective | How long can we be down? | 1 hour |

Everything else is chosen to meet these two numbers.

## Kinds of backups

| Kind | What | Restore |
|------|------|---------|
| **Logical** (`pg_dump`) | SQL or archive of the data | Slow for large databases; portable; good for single tables |
| **Physical snapshot** | Copy of the data files / disk snapshot | Fast to restore a whole database |
| **Continuous log archiving** (WAL archiving) | Every change streamed to storage | Enables **point-in-time recovery** |

## Point-in-time recovery (PITR)

```
Sunday full snapshot ──WAL──WAL──WAL──WAL──▶ now
                                  ▲
                          10:41:59 "oops, DELETE ran at 10:42"
restore = Sunday snapshot + replay WAL up to 10:41:59
```

With PITR, your RPO is about the WAL shipping interval (seconds to minutes), and you can rewind to **just before** the
mistake. Managed databases (RDS, Cloud SQL, Neon…) offer this out of the box.

## The 3-2-1 rule

- **3** copies of the data,
- on **2** different kinds of storage,
- **1** off-site, in another region or account, ideally immutable so a compromised account can't delete it.

Ransomware and a fat-fingered admin both delete backups too. Keep at least one copy that can't be modified for N days
(object lock).

## Untested backups don't exist

The most common backup failure is discovering, during an incident, that restores don't work: wrong permissions, a
missing WAL segment, an encryption key nobody has.

- **Restore regularly** (weekly) into a scratch environment, automatically.
- **Time it.** That's your real RTO.
- **Check the data** (row counts, latest timestamps).
- Write and practise the runbook.

## Faster RTO for big databases

Restoring 5 TB and replaying a day of WAL can take hours. Options:
- more frequent snapshots, so there's less WAL to replay;
- a **delayed replica** (applies changes 1 hour late), letting you stop it before the bad change and promote it;
- restoring only the damaged table from a logical export.

## Try it

An e-commerce company has: nightly `pg_dump` at 2 am kept on the same server's disk; two async replicas. A bug
deletes half the orders at 4 pm. What's lost, what's the RTO, and what would you change?

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

Replicas copied the delete, so they don't help. The latest backup is 2 am: **14 hours of orders lost** (RPO 14 h).
Restoring a large dump may take hours. If the server's disk dies, the backups die with it. Change to continuous WAL
archiving with PITR to off-site, immutable storage; daily snapshots; automated restore tests; maybe a delayed replica.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- Replicas aren't backups: they copy mistakes instantly.
- Set RPO and RTO first; design backups to meet them.
- PITR (snapshot + log archive) lets you rewind to just before a mistake.
- Follow 3-2-1 with an immutable off-site copy, and test restores regularly.
