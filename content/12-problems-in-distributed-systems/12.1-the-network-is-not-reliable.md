# The network is not reliable

On one machine, a function call either returns or throws. Across a network, there's a third outcome: **you don't
know.** That third outcome is the root of nearly every problem in this part of the course.

## Partial failure

Service A calls service B, and gets no reply within 2 s. Possibilities:

1. The request never reached B.
2. B received it and crashed before doing anything.
3. B did the work, and **crashed before replying**.
4. B did the work and replied; **the reply was lost**.
5. B is just slow and will reply in 3 s.

From A's side, **all five look identical.** If the operation was "charge ₹500", cases 3 and 4 mean the money moved.
Retrying blindly charges twice; not retrying may lose an order.

## The fallacies of distributed computing

Assumptions that feel true on a laptop and are false in production:

| Fallacy | Reality |
|---------|---------|
| The network is reliable | Packets drop, connections reset, links partition |
| Latency is zero | Every hop costs ms; across regions, 100+ ms |
| Bandwidth is infinite | Big payloads queue and congest |
| The network is secure | Encrypt and authenticate everything (mTLS) |
| Topology doesn't change | Instances, IPs and routes change constantly |
| There is one administrator | Many teams, providers and configs |
| Transport cost is zero | Serialisation, TLS and egress cost CPU and money |
| The network is homogeneous | Mixed hardware, versions, MTUs, middleboxes |

## Partitions

A **network partition** splits nodes into groups that can't talk to each other, while each group is still running and
still serving clients. It's rarely a cut cable; more often a misconfigured firewall, an overloaded switch, a GC pause,
or a cloud zone hiccup.

During a partition, a system must choose for each operation: **refuse** (stay consistent, lose availability) or
**proceed** (stay available, risk inconsistency). That's the core of CAP (Module 13).

## Gray failures

Worse than "down" is **half-working**: a node that answers health checks but times out on real work, a disk that's
10x slower, a link dropping 2% of packets. Simple health checks miss these. Outlier detection (lesson 4.5) and
latency-based checks catch them.

## Design principles

- **Every remote call can fail, or hang.** Use timeouts (12.2).
- **Every retried operation must be idempotent** (12.4).
- **Fail fast and isolate** when a dependency is unhealthy (12.3).
- **Don't trust wall clocks** across machines (12.5).
- **Prefer designs that tolerate staleness** over designs that need perfect coordination.

## Try it

A payment service calls a bank API. The call times out after 5 s. List what may have happened at the bank, and what
the payment service should do next.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

The bank may have: never received it; received it and failed; charged the card but its reply was lost; or still be
processing. The service should **not** blindly retry with a new request. Retry with the **same idempotency key**, or
query the bank for the payment's status by that key, and keep the order in a "pending" state until the outcome is
known.
</details>

## Key takeaways

- A remote call can succeed, fail, or leave you not knowing, and "not knowing" is common.
- The fallacies of distributed computing are traps; design against them.
- Partitions and gray failures happen; health checks must catch half-working nodes.
- Timeouts, idempotency and isolation are the basic defences.
